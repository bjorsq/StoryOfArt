In the twenty first century it is thought to be difficult to define art. It does not always exist in a gallery. It is not always a solid object or a flat image. It can be imagined, an action, a pledge, existing only temporarily, it can be made by one people or many, it can be an idea which accumulates over time. It can exist in other realities and will exist in realities of which we do not yet know. It is thought not to be the servant of the state, of religion or of ritual, but to exist purely for its own sake. This subjective freedom is highly valued and often disputed. The one constant is that art is made by artists and that it is made for its own sake. It was not always this way.
!!THE BEGINNING OF ART.
The story of art is the story of the human urge to represent the world. This began before humans had acquired verbal language with the simple drawing of a line in the sand with a finger. In 6000 BC the indigenous groups of prehistoric humans in the continents of Europe, Africa and Asia, the Middle East, the Far East, Australasian and the Americas had not developed any forms of verbal or visual communication. There was no form of representation in the world. But as these peoples evolved and began to acquire language, conceptual and cognitive skills, they began to create tools. The first tool may have been a stick used to draw in the sand, or it may have been a stone used to crack a nut or to kill an animal. 
!!PREHISTORIC ART.
Tools and visual representations are the first evidence we have of the existence of early civilisations. The earliest examples of these have been found in Africa, the home of the Neanderthals from whom all humans are descended. Prehistoric people were developing forms of representation before the discovery of fire. We know this because some of their drawings and tools have survived. Art, or the evidence of people’s urge to record their life, is the oldest surviving evidence of human endeavour. 
It may be the case that music, probably in the form of rhythm, is an older form of human expression but only drawing, artefacts and objects have permanence. This is why they are assigned such great value in western culture where linear history is the primary means of self-definition.
Many other cultures do not place the same emphasis on history. Their artefacts and art are different.
In the beginning art may have been pure representation. We do not know whether there was a purpose to art other than pure self expression because there are many examples of prehistoric art whose purpose is unknown to us. There are lines drawn in the desert in south America that form geometric patterns visible only from the air, patterns scratched and carved into rock, and the countless temporary forms of expression that must have been washed or blown away.
!!THE STONE AGE. 
As humans evolved, their visual representations became part of the rituals they created around the most important aspects of life, fertility, birth, death and the hunting of food.
Art began to have a specific function. The earliest surviving images are scratched into stone, and consist mostly of lines, for example the cave paintings at Lascaux in France. These and others like them depict people hunting animals, in the case of Lascaux a buffalo like creature. The paintings are drawn in outline on the wall using paints made of earth and soot based pigments, in ochre, reddish brown and black. There are groups of lines too which are unexplained but seem to function as a way of counting or as a visual dynamic. 
As prehistoric people developed more sophisticated tools, they discovered a greater variety of ways of making marks. Flints could be chipped to make sharp blades with which to cut animal skin, animal hair, and to pulverise plants, earth and clay. The discovery of fire meant that substances for drawing, painting and modelling could be dried, heated, reduced, distilled and fire provided soot, ash charcoal and light. 
!!THE IRON AGE
Iron age people had a much greater range of tools and implements. They had begun to fashion utensils and to make personal adornments from metal, as well as tools and weapons and with these came the development of decoration. Objects began to have greater value and the degree and type of their decoration began to be significant. At the same time Iron age people also began to acquire language.
One of the first groups to produce metal vessels for eating and drinking was the Beaker people. Often these objects were decorated with images of animals, vitally important as a source of food.
!!ABORIGINAL ART.
In Australasia, the aboriginal people developed an entirely different form of representation. Early aboriginal art was a language of symbolic representation in which pattern and symbol is used to represent the world. Drawings were made using different colour of sand and earth straight onto the ground. These described the land and its spirits, and depicted time as a journey. The drawings were temporary and not always shown to everybody, sometimes even being made in isolation.
Different cultures developed different views of the world according to their geographical location, the climate and the food that was available to them. During the Iron age people from other continents developed along different paths leading to a material expression of cultural  difference.
!!THE BRONZE AGE.
The smelting of metals led to the discovery of a range of alloys, the first being bronze. These metals could be fashioned more intricately and finely allowing for greater precision and decorative effect such as repeating patterns, beaten metal and thin wire.
!!EARLY CIVILISATIONS. 
The earliest civilisations were formed as people began to travel further in search of food and hospitable land and to encounter other groups both aggressively and peaceably. Humans had previously been nomadic but some groups began to make permanent settlements and with these came buildings and monuments. Cave painting and rock carving had been the only permanent sites for art until the construction of permanent dwellings. Settlement gave rise to the construction of stone buildings, burial chambers and monuments all of which could be decorated or contain representation. For nomadic people portability had been a factor but now larger and heavier objects were produced and people began to make figurative sculptural representations from stone, clay and wood. 
Stonehenge, the great stone circle in Britain which was built in the Stone Age to celebrate the solstice, is a construction which is not decorated but is not a dwelling. It is an early church. Subsequently buildings which were the focus for ritual whether pagan or religious became the most highly decorated anywhere. Art and architecture began to be closely linked.
After the Bronze Age the great early civilisations of the world developed in isolation, reaching their peaks in terms of population, claims to territory and cultural attainment at different periods in history. 
!Pre-Columbian art.
The pre-Columbian people in south America made some of the most sophisticated early sculpture. Their stone male and female figures have distinctive flattened and squared forms, with large featured faces. 
Later in south America the Mayan Aztec and Inca civilisations became very skilful in the use of gold. 

In Africa, the Sumerians and Abyssinians and many other cultures developed widely different forms of visual expression, both in the adornment of the body and dwellings, and in the production of artefacts. 
!!TRIBAL ART.
In all the continents of the world large numbers of people continued to live as tribes, some settled and some nomadic, rarely coming into contact with other peoples and each with its own cultural specificity. Native Americans, semi-nomadic peoples who lived off the plains of America, used leather, beads, feathers and wood, and are today customarily symbolised by the Totem pole, a ritual sculpture. Melanesian and Polynesian peoples that depended on the sea for their livelihood used wood, shells and bark and pounded tree bark to make the tapa cloth which they printed with symbolic designs. Their boats were adorned with the work of their most skilled artists. The Inuit who inhabited the frozen North of America carved from soft stone. The African subcontinent yielded the largest number of distinct tribes, for example the Nuba, Yoruba, Dogon, Masai and Zulu. Africa was one of the culturally richest parts of the world. 
!!EMPIRES.
The earliest civilisations soon built cities and with cities came armies and battles for territory. Soon empires were formed the earliest being the Byzantine, Ottoman, the Roman empire the Japanese and Chinese empires which were ruled by successive dynasties. 
Greece is often referred to as the cradle of civilisation and early Greek culture was the most sophisticated in the world.
The development of religions was very significant in terms of the history of art was since these became the focus of a large proportion of artistic activity.
!! ART and RELIGION.
Religions divided the world and generated an unprecedented quantity of visual art. Many parts of the world remained pagan, worshipping local gods with their own iconography and ritual. The Greeks, for example, had a complex family of pagan gods and myths. Larger religions began to spread: in the east, Islam, the religion of the Ottoman empire predominated, and in the middle east Christianity. In Asia Hinduism and Islam co-existed although not necessarily in harmony and in China and Japan Buddhism and Taoism were predominant. Many Northern Europeans such as the Celts, Norse and the Vikings, were pagans. A burial ship found at Sutton Hoo in Britain contained many examples of Viking art. The ancient Egyptians had their own gods. 
 
Art had been an important part of ritual. It now became a servant of the state and of religion and in the process the works that have become known as some of the greatest works of art were created. Artists became the producers of religious iconography but of course in Islamic part areas no figurative representation appeared anywhere since Islam does not permit it
!!EARLY WRITTEN TEXT.
Writing is a very important influence on art during the period leading up to the birth of Christ and afterward. It is thought that writing was developed by the Sumerians and that Sanskrit may be the oldest written language. The earliest surviving artefacts are the Dead Sea scrolls, written in Sanskrit on papyrus, and the Rosetta stone, an inscribed tablet. 
Writing was a new way of describing and recording the world which had previously been exclusively visual and for centuries writing was a skill learned only by the very few. Visual imagery remained the primary means of communicating and recording events, but as writing began to tip the balance, art was led to fulfil new functions.
!!EGYPTIAN ART.
The ancient Egyptians developed an early writing system called hieroglyphics They were a wealthy trading civilisation with a highly sophisticated system of government and constructed some of the greatest buildings and monuments ever made, the Pyramids, the Sphinx and the statues of the pharaohs in the Valley of the Kings. The Egyptians worshipped deities such as Isis and Osiris and held cats, certain birds and scarab beetles to be sacred. 
Egyptian art is seen at is most spectacular in the tombs of the pharaohs. They believed in the afterlife, therefore the art that has been found in Egyptian tombs is a good indicator of the life of the living. Egyptian artists were skilled in carving, casting and gold and silver smithing. They had a style of drawing that is very distinctive – all figures are shown with their body facing forward and the head in profile. Hieroglyphs use this style too in the creation of long vertically written texts consisting of pictograms. Egyptian two dimensional art emphasises flatness and uses no perspective but in contrast Egyptian sculpture is very realistic. A cat  has been found, carved from black stone wearing a jewelled collar, for example and countless sarcophagi for mummified pharaohs bear lifelike effigies. The sphinxes and the pharaohs carved on a monumental scale, forty metres high, are in proportion and physiologically accurate.
!!ETRUSCAN ART.
Etruscan pottery is known for the quality of its form and decoration.
!!BYZANTINE ART.
The Byzantine empire was one of the largest empires in the world. Byzantine art is valued for the quality of figurative drawing and painting. which had developed physiological accuracy, realism and early use of perspective. 
!!GRECO ROMAN ART.
around 400 BC the Greeks had developed a civilisation that was pre-eminent. The Greek system of government was the model for all modern nations. The Greeks had a written language, and a flourishing culture. Greek sculpture was the most realistic in the world, and it was at this time that the idea that art should aspire to realism began to take root. 
Greek sculptures were generally of white marble or other stone, and often painted in bright colours. The best known Greek sculptures are the Elgin marbles, subject of a protracted battle between Britain, who has possession of them and Greece, who wants them back. The marbles once adorned the frieze of one of the most famous buildings of antiquity, the Parthenon in Athens. 
The Venus de Milo, a classical Greek sculpture, is perhaps the best known sculpture of the western world, considered to represent perfection of female form and becoming a byword for artistic skill and for beauty. It is made in white marble and at some point since antiquity has suffered the loss of its arms. 
Greek vases are very finely decorated by incising into the black glaze which covers the red clay from which they are made. They are renowned for the quality and realism of the drawing.
Greek art was for centuries to be seen as the benchmark for all art, and Classical Greece provided the model for the Roman empire, whose cultural achievements dominated Europe for centuries after the Empire fell. The Romans built cities, roads, and infrastructure on a vast scale and created art to decorate their empire and venerate their leaders. The carved stone Roman portrait bust is a good example, of which there are countless examples surviving. Many archaeological sites have revealed the variety of roman art, for example Herculaneum, Pompeii, two towns which were destroyed but preserved fro centuries by eh eruption of the volcano Vesuvius. Mosaic wall decorations and paintings revealed after excavation in the nineteenth century show mosaic floor designs featuring food and fish, and murals depicting sports such as leaping over a bulls horns and dolphins.
Knossos in Crete also has excellent murals, whilst Volubilis and Lullingstone Villa in Britain contain similar but smaller scale work.
Roman art spread so widely that it became a dominant style, Romanesque, as artists, artisans and craftspeople adapted and personalized it. Many cathedrals and churches in Europe were built in this style for the next 200 hundred years as Catholicism developed.
!!ISLAMIC ART.
At the same time in the middle east and in Asia, Islam spread, bringing its own cultural identity to the towns and cities of the Ottoman empire. Islam demanded that there be no figurative representation of any kind, whether in architectural decoration, textiles, book, fabric, painting or sculpture. This was because the word by which the teachings of Mohammed had been handed down, was itself sacred, and so the word became the only permissible imagery. Written text was rendered as decorative design, carving and patter, and calligraphic patterns of remarkable complexity were carved into stone to adorn mosques for example. Carpets for which the area then known as Persia became so famous, depicted animals, plants and buildings symbolically. For centuries there was no art produced in Islamic parts of the world that represented a human face.
!!CHINESE ART 
By contrast in the far east, home of Buddhism, representational painting and sculpture featured amongst the highest achievements of artists. Japan and China have produced the finest clay sculpture in the world, of example the 
Glazed clay horses of the Tang dynasty and the terracotta army found buried in the late twentieth century. This consists of hundreds of statues of warriors buried in a shallow underground tomb.
Chinese writing is not pictographic but the characters have a visual element. Chinese art therefore has a strong tradition in calligraphic art. Chinese watercolours have calligraphic brushstrokes made quickly which represent plants, landscape, particularly mountains, and sometimes animals and birds. Chinese paintings were made on silk which was readily available rather than canvas. painting on silk required soft small brushes and thin paint.
Since Buddhism did not depend on graphic illustration, religious iconography never became an intrinsic part of daily life. However Buddha was widely represented in statue form, from the smallest pocket carving to the largest stone or metal figures such as the reclining gold Buddha that is still famous today.
!!JAPANESE ART.
Clay and porcelain modelling is an important element of Japanese art, seen in the ceramics of the Sung and Ming eras which reached a pinnacle of excellence which was not surpassed anywhere else. Japanese ceramics are famous all over the world and their influence continues to be seen in contemporary ceramics and mass produced porcelain both in the form and in the decoration. Japanese ceramics were an intrinsic part of the tea ceremony, an important daily ritual in Japanese life.
Japanese watercolours and woodblocks are well known too. The Japanese were skilled in the making of paper and the production of silk. Although Buddhism has its place in Japan, Confucianism was widely followed, and this was more a philosophical teaching than a religion. No religious iconography was attached to Confucianism.
The opposing but complementary and essential principles of Yin and yang influence much Japanese art. They can be seen in the many representations of landscape and weather, in particular rocky mountains with stylised swirling wind and cloud and in similar sculptures which are large chunks of rock with especially appreciated forms.
Japanese art has a flatness to it, a lack of deep perspective, that has prevailed for centuries. The famous willow pattern, a Victorian interpretation of traditional Japanese art, shows a never-ending landscape that exists all on one plane. 
!!CHRISTIAN ART.
After the spread of Christianity a new kind of art began to emerge. Christianity was an iconographic religion based on the worship of a god with a human form, his son, Jesus a human and Mary his mother. The depiction of these three has given rise to some of the greatest and most influential art in the western world. For a period of several hundred years, from the Medieval period to the seventeenth century and the Enlightenment, most significant art had a religious theme or was commissioned by the Church. The rulers of Europe were considered to rule by divine right, putting art also into the service of the state. 
Early Christian art, such as the Coptic tapestries, the earliest surviving woven textiles that depict Christ and Mary, is simple and shows images of the face of Christ. Wherever Christianity spread, local artists styles were put to the service of the church, resulting in multiple representations of the same three people with local regional and ethnic features. But there are constant elements – God is usually shown as an old man with a white beard, Christ as a young man with long brown hair and a beard and Mary as a young woman with long black or brown hair. 
Judaism, like Islam, did not permit representational in any form, its teachings being transmitted through words and closely prescribed laws governing everyday life. So that for a time early representational art was essentially a Christian endeavour. In the early medieval period many churches had fresco painting in them and these frescos are some of the earliest surviving paintings that we have today.
During the period in which Islam, Christianity and Judaism were expanding, very few people were literate apart from the clergy and members of the ruling classes. Visual imagery was still the primary means of mass communication. The Bayeux tapestry, as late as the eleventh century was made as a piece of news reportage after the fact to tell the story of the battle of Hastings through images. These images acted as a prompt for the spoken word – a storyteller would stand in the hall in which the tapestry hung around all four walls and tell the tale. The diagonal marks in the borders of the tapestry are thought to add some kind of rthymic dynamic to the spoken word.
With the invention of printing and the dissemination of the Bible, literacy improved. The dissemination of visual images through print was more democratic.
!!MEDIEVAL ART. 
In the Middle Ages, the influence of the Romans was still pervasive. Romanesque and Gothic art and architecture dominated northern Europe, but towards the south the influence of the moors was seen in Spanish and Portuguese art. In the east Islamic art and architecture mixed with pockets of Christianity and Judaism led to a non-representational art, but in the far east in china Japan and in Russia, visual art was representational.
! Lucas Cranach.
The work of Lucas Cranach, a German medieval artist working in the 1300s is a good example of northern European religious art. Cranach painted an altarpiece that was shocking for the time since it accurately depicted the misery suffered by Christ on the cross, the pain and the physical degradation. Cranach also showed the plight of ordinary people of the  time by painting them with festering sores, deformities and other afflictions. He painted very realistically in quite bright colours.
!Matthias Grunewald.
Grunewald, a German medieval artist working around 1350 is also known for his crucifixion scenes and for the gritty realism of his paintings, extraordinary for the time, and for his use of colour.
!Hieronymous Bosch.
Another German painter, Bosch, painted a version of heaven called The Garden of Earthly Delights. This extremely well known painting is a small triptych approximately 70 cm tall with a square central panel and two rectangular side panels. Bosch's painting depicts paradise as an extraordinary place, with dreamlike creatures and objects, some beautiful pink glass spheres, some castles and towers. It is an early precursors to fantasy and science fiction illustration, being full of fantastical creations of Bosch’s imagination. The painting is very detailed in its composition and execution. 
! Peter Breughel the Elder.
Breughel’s work depicts medieval everyday life. A Dutch painter working in the 1400s, Breughel is known for his instantly recognisable paintings of the seasons, for example Hunters in the Snow. In each case the painting shows a village scene, with a large number of peasants, children and domestic animals going about their everyday life inside and outside buildings and dwellings . There is activity in every corner of the painting, down to the smallest detail such as a person urinating, or a dog stealing some meat, a couple kissing, children being naughty, someone sleeping. The scenes are realistically painted and are notable for their depiction of the lives of ordinary people with no imposed narrative, no moral or religious overtones, no theme other than the representation of life as it was. The colours, the drawing of landscape, vernacular, architecture, the detail of the clothing, of tools, house hold items, makes these works extraordinary documents of their time. Above all Breughel’s paintings of the seasons present four very different atmospheres and moods. The paintings are so famous as to be instantly recognisable whether or not the Breughels’s name is known, and are used on calendars, merchandise and in advertising the world over. 
! Peter Breughel the younger.
A Dutch painter related to Breughel the Elder
In the south of Europe, especially in Italy, painters were developing new ways of working. Many such as Piero di Cosimo, Antonello da Messina, Fra Angelico and Piero della Francesca were making frescos or wall paintings in monasteries and churches. 
! Antonello Da Messina.
Da Messina was an Italian renaissance painter who made a painting of Saint Jerome in his study. This shows the room as a box like structure that sits on its own in a landscape, giving the viewer access through the open front wall. What we are seeing is monk’s cell, consisting of two rooms linked made of dark coloured wood in one of which the saint is seated at a desk.
! Piero Della Francesca. 
This Siennese painter is perhaps the best known of the Italian late medieval period. In the 1450s Della Francesca painted a number of notable frescos in churches in central Italy, in particular an image of the Virgin Mary, pregnant and standing in a landscape with a tree, that can be seen, significantly restored in a tiny village near Siena. His painting of the baptism of Christ shows Christ being baptised by john the Baptist who is standing in a stream wearing a cloth round his loins. The Italian countryside, representing the Middle East, stretches away in the background. These artists understood how to use foreground and background to create a sense of three dimensional space. 

!!THE RENAISSANCE.
The fifteenth century saw a flowering of western culture centred around Italy. The dominance of the Catholic church and the wealth of the ruling families like the Medicis, led to a great number of artists being commissioned to make religious paintings for countless churches and portraits. The artists of this period are acknowledged to be among the greatest in the Western world. 
Italian artists like the painters Lorenzo de Ghiberti, Fillipo Lippi, Lorenzo Lotto, Lorenzo Ghiberti and Simone Martini made many paitings depicted scenes from the life of Christ set in the Italian countryside and with Italia architecture and clothes. 
!Lorenzetti.
Lorenzetti’s Good and Bad Government can be seen today in Siena. This painting is the width of a room, situated high up near the ceiling and it shows a fortified city on a hill in the distance with its people going about their daily lives. A man on a horse is riding into the image from left in the foreground, the horse has a white coat on and the man is wearing armour. Good and Bad Government is unusual for its depiction of an entire community and its attempt to represent democracy in action in a painting.
!Giorgione.
Giorgione was an Italian artist who painted The Flagellation of St Jerome, depicting the middle aged saint, with a thin sagging body, long grey hair and a beard, alone in penitent pose in his room, naked save for a red cloth around his loins. Jerome is surrounded by symbolic objects and is in the act of scourging himself with a long leather whip
During the Renaissance artist began to study technique and materials with a scientific approach. New pigments, painting techniques and materials were discovered and casting techniques for sculpture were refined. 
!!THE AGE OF PATRONAGE.
The renaissance was the great age of patronage. Artists who were commissioned by the Church or the State were in a position to afford assistants and to establish studios. They took on apprentices who were employed to draw out paintings, mix colours, prepare canvases, under paint, and often to paint backgrounds - often painting trees, grass, or patterns on fabric. Many old masters are in fact the work of a number of people which has sometimes made attribution difficult. An artists assistant might also go on to set up their own studio, and to paint in a very similar style to that of their master, so that it has often been difficult to tell who painted what. If a the painter of a particular work could not be established it was often attributed as being painted in a style similar to, or ‘after’ a certain master painter. 
!Mantegna Andrea.
Mantegna, an Italian renaissance painter is known for his many great paintings and for his invention of the painting technique grisaille.
!!PERSPECTIVE.
The discovery during the renaissance which had the most significant effect on painting was that of perspective. This was a landmark in the history of visual representation since it enabled artists to depict three dimensional space for the first time.
There were many theories of perspective but the most significant element was the establishment of the vanishing point. This was an imaginary point in the vertical centre of the canvas, about one third down from the top, at which all sightlines from the position of the viewer converged, giving an illusion of three dimensional space and in particular, perspective enabled a far greater accuracy in the depiction of the built environment. Complex theories tried to show that there could be a perspective that enabled the illusion to be effective from every viewing angle. Perspective was essentially an illusion constructed through geometry but in the Renaissance it was thought to be a two dimensional replication of what the human eye actually saw. 
! Leonardo Da Vinci.
One renaissance artist blended science and art more successfully than any others. Leonardo da Vinci is often seen as the quintessential Renaissance Man who defined the era. He worked as a court artist for the Medici family in Venice and did not paint only religious subjects. Da Vinci is arguably the most famous artist in the world, or has certainly made the most famous painting, the Mona Lisa, which has been reproduced by the billion, forged and stolen many times and still draws huge crowds to its site on the walls of the Louver museum in Paris. This painting depicts a woman shown head and shoulders turned very slightly to one side. She has on a simple yellowish gold dress a plain necklace and has brown hair. She does not look particularly wealthy. There is a landscape in the background. There is an inscription LHOQ below her on a painted scroll. 
What made the painting so famous is the woman’s expression, in particular her smile. She seems to be looking straight at the viewer, and has a look of cool calm appraisal, the hint of a smile on her lips. However her eyes seem to be fixed on the viewer wherever they stand in relation the painting, leading to the myth that the painting is actually following the movements of her viewers, that she is somehow real. This is an extraordinary achievement for a painting which has come to symbolise great beauty. The term Mona Lisa has almost become an adjective to describe a beautiful woman
The myth attached to the painting has led to many copies being made and to the work featuring in other artists work, in advertising, in newspaper cartoons, in fact it is an image that has currency everywhere in the Western World and often beyond. Mona Lisa copies appear in fairgrounds with holes cut for heads so that people can be photographed as her. Automatic photo booths have the Mona Lisa as a backdrop or as a body for the sitters head shot. Copies of her with the eyes cut out have been used in films for comedic effect – someone behind the painting will look through the eye hole so that it appears that she is following a person around the room and Marcel Duchamp famously graffitied a moustache onto a reproduction of her.
The author of this image succeeded in painting a very beautiful portrait, but it is only one of his achievements. He made many religious paintings and altarpieces. A cartoon for a Madonna and child is particularly well known. He did a vast number of drawings from the models, paying endless attention to hands and feet, eyes or other parts of the body, drawing them separately to get them right. 
Da Vinci was a polymath and his interests extended to anatomy and engineering. He performed many dissections and recorded what he saw in drawings, expanding his sense of how to draw the human form this way as well as his medical knowledge of how the human body worked even drawing a foetus still inside a woman’s uterus.
Another famous work by Da Vinci is a pen and ink drawing called The Golden Section. A male figure with flowing hair is drawn standing with both arms stretched out horizontally and one leg pointed outwards at an angle. Measured lines radiate from the figure, which represents the proportions taken from the human body used to make perspective and on which architectural construction is based. A copy of this drawing was placed on the Moon by the crew of the Apollo in the 1960s.
Da Vinci was also an inventor and was so interested in the myth of Icarus that he built a flying machine.
!Sandro Botticelli.
Another well known Italian painter working around 1540 is best known for his painting The Nativity and The Birth of Venus, and also for his illustrations to Dante’s Inferno. Botticelli’s The Birth of Venus is one of the most recognised images in the western world due to its frequent use in advertising. The very beautiful Venus is standing in a giant clam shell which floats on the sea, just away from the shore. She is naked but her long hair covers her pubic area. She is surrounded by cherubs who pull the clam shell along by blue ribbons. Pale pink roses fall around her from the sky. The whole painting is rendered in luminous pale colours, blues, pinks and sand colours. 
The Nativity a small painting about 50 cm tall is certainly one of the best known nativity scenes ever painted. The stable is set in a forested landscape, with a timber and straw shelter in the centre of the picture which houses the Holy family. The shelter has no walls. The Mary is seated with Jesus on her lap. Joseph kneels beside her. The shepherds are there. The ox and ass are in the shadows at the back of the stable. Above the thatched roof are angels, arranged in a row, some blowing trumpets or playing harps, their feet dangling, their softly coloured red blue and purple robes falling in folds around them, with gilded belts and halos hovering. Above the angels heaven can be seen though breaks in the clouds, and there are cherubs everywhere. Below the stable is a twisting path with leads to the bottom of the painting crossing horizontally, turning again and again, until the flames at the mouth of hell are reached. All along the path are devils poking out of burrows enticing lost souls, they have black skin, horns and forked tails.
A similar but smaller work, The Birth of Ezra, predates this painting.  
Botticelli made a series of drawings to illustrate Dante’s Inferno. These show the different levels of purgatory and the sins associated with each level and graphically illustrate the particular torture or punishment inflicted on the lost souls. The route to heaven is then shown culminating in the entry in to heaven, which is a beautiful garden. The drawings are made for a book, done in dark brown ink and are very detailed.
!Van Eyck.
Around the same time, Van Eyck ho made a painting called the Arnolfini Marriage which is particularly well known. The painting shows a wedding couple standing side by side. The woman is wearing a long dark green dress and holds one hand up, palm toward the viewer. There is a landscape in the background.
Sculpture also developed rapidly in the renaissance. Artists began to measure their models with callipers and develop tools, metal alloys and casting and carving methods that enabled them to create realistic simulations of the human form. Sculpture studios had enough employees to enable the handling of large blocks of stone and the construction of foundries.
!Donatello.
Donatello is one of the greatest renaissance sculptors. 
! Benvenuto Cellini.
An Italian renaissance sculptor and goldsmith, Cellini was working around 1540. His made a bronze statue of David with which he attempted to rival Michelangelo and also made a bronze of Medusa. Cellini was well respected for his skill as a goldsmith and his work was much in demand with the Medici family. An extravagant gold salt cellar in the form of an ornate clam shell containing Neptune and many sea nymphs is particularly well known. The salt cellar was stolen in 2003. Cellini wrote an outspoken and lengthy autobiography which details the rivalry between artists’ studios., 
!Michelangelo.
The greatest renaissance sculpture is generally acknowledged to be a figure of the biblical David which was made by the sculpture and painter Michelangelo to represent ideal male beauty. This white marble figure of a nude man is larger than life, about 6 metres high and was radical at the time for its naturalism and the physiological accuracy with which it was modelled. Michelangelo applied the lessons of perspective to the sculpture, making the feet proportionally larger than they should have been. Several smaller versions were made from bronze.
This sculpture was much copied and inspired much debate in the studios of Florence.
Michelangelo was an Italian artist working in Florence who is universally known as one of the greatest artists of all time, his name often being used to invoke the idea of the highest achievement in art. Michelangelo is as well known for his fresco on the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel showing the life of Christ and the ascension into heaven. He was a very skilled draughtsman and created very lifelike figures using colour in a very realistic way and painting flowing drapery. The Sistine chapel ceiling was an enormous undertaking and to paint it Michelangelo and his assistants spent a year lying on their backs on scaffolding towers. 
Images from the ceiling are known all over the world, often used by advertisers, and have become almost generic. One part of the fresco is most frequently reproduced, the image of Christ’s outstretched hand reaching out to the hand of God, their fingers nearly touching. This is an image of great tension and portent, and it seems as if an electrical charge passes from one to the other through their fingers. In the late 1990s the ceiling was cleaned, provoking a great deal of controversy since for centuries the fresco had been darkened and the colours muted. The fresco had been known to visitors and to those looking at reproductions as an antique, parts of which were even obscured from view by centuries of dirt and the breath of millions of visitors. The cleaned ceiling was very brightly coloured even garish. Michelangelo also made a marble sculpture of the Pieta.
By the end of the fifteenth century the fame of Italian masters had spread throughout Europe. Artists began to look beyond religious and political subjects to the myths classical Greece which were sometimes used as metaphors for contemporary events. Classicism was the dominant theme for artists in the sixteenth century such as Poussin, El Greco, Raphael and Titian. 
Very few artists were women. Artemesia Gentileschi was a rare exception although there may have been more of whom there is no record.
!Artemisia Gentileschi.
An Italian artist who is the best known woman painter of the classical period, Gentileschi’s most frequently cited work is a portrait of a woman and a painting of the Martyrdom of St Catherine.
!El Greco.
Most of the well known artists of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries are Italian or Spanish. El Greco, ‘the Greek’, is an exception. He made paintings using loose brushstrokes with bold colours.

!Caravaggio.
Caravaggio is another classical Italian painter. He painted in a more precise and well worked way than Titian, for example, and was known for his complex compositions. Caravaggio invented chiaroscuro, a way of painting using only the lightness and darkness in a scene. One painting by him shows a scene of bacchanalian revelry, a number of semi-clad young men wearing ivy wreaths in their hair. 
!Poussin Nicholas.
A French classical artist who made precisely drawn and richly coloured paintings, Poussin’s work has a theatrical atmosphere, the figures in his paintings are painted in very bright colours and posed to look as if they are immobile and the scene he depicts often looks staged rather than natural. For this reason he fell out of favour for some time and not much was known about him. Poussin was rediscovered in the early twentieth century by the art historian Anthony Blunt who wrote a catalogue raisonné.
!Bernini.
Italian baroque painter, made many altarpieces particularly in Venice and Verona, working in the mid 17th century

!!CLASSICAL REALISM.
!Raphael.
An Italian sixteenth century painter who was influential for his loose and impressionistic way of painting and use of colour, unusual for the period. Raphael was commissioned to make many paintings for churches in Florence. He made a lot of working drawings and drew cartoons from which his paintings were traced.
!Titian.
Titian is a very influential Italian painter who made many altarpieces for churches in Florence and Rome but also use classical themes which gave him greater freedom of interpretation and therefore the space to experiment. Titian’s style was much emulated, leading to many works being wrongly attributed to him in 20th century. He developed a style of painting that, like Raphael’s, was looser and more impressionistic than his contemporaries. Titiian began to show that the surface of the painting and the way the paint is used could be just as important for the viewer as the subject of the painting. It became important that the individuality and singularity of the painter’s touch should be celebrated. Artists began to make paintings that were far more than a presentation of a narrative subject realising that the way paint was applied could reveal as much of the psychology of the subject as could the literal visual interpretation. 
Titian was one of the pioneers of this way of working, displayed in paintings like ‘The Flaying of Marsyus’, a 4 metre high painting which shows Marsyus tied to a tree as his flesh is cut off by several men in a crowd. Dogs at his feet feed on his flesh. 
Titian was also a great colourist and used daring and vivid combinations as can be seen in a crucifixion painted for the cathedral in the Italian city of Ancona. This shows his compositional skill too – like many painters of the time Titian controlled the direction of the viewers eye by carefully considered compositional elements, such as the twist of a body, the direction of the gaze of the subjects, the direction of a pointed arm, the flow of drapery and the linking of  areas of similar colour. Diagonal trajectories were popular, so that the viewer might be led into the centre of a painting, sometimes these became spirals that take the viewers eye in several steps to the focal point of the composition. The use of red of another bright colour often indicated the focal point.
Titian often depicted Japanese or Chinese ceramics in his interiors, evidence that these were newly imported and popular in Italy at the time. 
!REALISM.
During the sixteenth century as the European nations vied for power, their monarchies became the main patrons of art. Court artists such as Velasquez painted the ruling families of Europe and had the time and financial backing to break new ground. Artists such as Velasquez, Chardin, or Van Dyck for example began to depict real life rather than fictional scenes, mythical or religious subjects, going beyond the formal portrait to paint events, objects and people that interested them. History painting and still life, were explored by an increasing number of painters
!Diego Velasquez.
This Spanish century painter appointed to the Spanish court painted some of the best known works in the history of painting. One of his most popular works shows the King Philip and the queen, the Infanta standing in an informal group with various servants in a room in the royal household looking out at the viewer. Velasquez chose to show the family at home in intimate surroundings, several rooms beyond the room in which they stand visible through a door behind them. The family look out of the painting as if the  viewer has trespassed upon them in their private quarters yet they regard the viewer with a challenging cool assurance. The use of perspective and light and shade focuses the viewer’s eye on the Infanta. This painting does not have a literal narrative but neither is it a straight forward family portrait. Velasquez has taken a psychological approach to his subjects which was unusual at the time.
Velazquez also painted many portraits including one of King Philip on a rearing horse, and a portrait of the Infanta wearing a lacy dress. The detail of the spectacular red dress, the lace, embroidery and the jewels on it, is painted with thick squeezes of paint which viewed close up seem no more than rapid impressions and the viewer is very conscious of the physical properties of paint on canvas. Viewed from distance however the apparently crude application of paint is astoundingly like the fabric it portrays.  
The Rokeby Venus is one of Velasquez’s best known works. It shows a reclining nude with her back to the viewer, her face, which stares directly at the viewer, visible in a mirror held in front of her on ribbons by a cherub with red and white feathered wings. This painting, like the one of the royal family, addresses the psychology of looking at paintings. It gives the viewer access to the subject but ensures by the choice of pose, composition and facial expression that the subject is portrayed as being aware that they are being observed. This ensures that the viewer cannot avoid acknowledging their own subjectivity.
!!THE STILL LIFE.
Artists had begun to explore new subjects and the still life became an important genre of painting which reached its height in sixteenth and seventeenth century Europe. The still life had many forms, the most common being a table bearing an array of food and drink, a vase of flowers, or an arrangement of objects. The still life allowed the painter to display skill in the way the various items are painted and can be seen essentially as a close up view, in contrast to a painting of a scene or a person. The skin of a lemon, the shine and reflection on silver ware, the pattern on a pheasants feathers, the weave of a rug, the petals of flowers all gave ample opportunity for displays of virtuosity.
A typical food still life might have fruit piled casually on a plate, silver pitchers or goblets, a dead game bird, a candle, knives, glasses, some meat or fish. The food would usually have been chosen to present an impressive display so that anyone who saw the picture would be impressed by how the owner was able to live. Zubaran and Chardin were early exponents of the still life.
!Zubaran.
A Spanish sixteenth century artist who is known chiefly for his still lifes, Zubaran made several paintings of vegetables such as cabbages and pumpkins. In one such painting an array of vegetables is shown hanging from a line, tied on by string of varying lengths. The vegetables are set against a plain background.
!Chardin.
Like Zubaran, Chardin usually painted flowers and vegetables and was rigorous in his choice of subject and composition, often making very simple arrangements of one or two items and concentrating on painting them as realistically as possible.
Similarly, paintings of flowers were a signal of wealth and many artists added flowers to combinations of other items. Later on the Dutch flower painters concentrated on the flowers alone, filling the canvas with vases of exotic mixed floral arrangements. These were also intended as displays of wealth, for only the richest could afford cut flowers. 
Some still life painting depicted only objects, perhaps a rustic plate and cup, a book and a lamp, or richly decorated precious items. Kalf, a Dutch artist, painted a suit of armour cast onto a chair by a table covered with gold goblets and plates, the metal glows and shines and reflects fire and lamplight. Kalf’s virtuosity is revealed by a close inspection of the painted shine, made from the tiniest and most carefully applied touches of a range of yellows, whites and ochre.
Whereas most still life paintings have no narrative, the vanitas painting is a form of still life which depicts objects with an allegorical or symbolic significance. The vanitas might be painted to celebrate a particular persons interests and achievements through objects alone, or combined with a portrait of the person. 
The symbolism in vanitas painting is not always evident and some knowledge is required to interpret them. skulls make an obvious and frequent appearance to represent mortality, as do books to represent learning, musical instruments, globes, sextants and telescopes to represent travel and the outside world. However more obscure and personal items would have been significant only to the subject or commissioner of the painting and those that knew them. 
One of the best known vanitas paintings is Holbein's The Ambassadors. 
!Holbein.
An English artist working during the reign of Elizabeth the first, Holbein, like most artists of the time mostly painted to commission, recording the members of and activities of the royal Court. He made several distinctive standing portraits of the Queen including one in which she is wearing a white embroidered dress decorated with pearls, one of her father Henry VIII, and many other people, usually between 50 cm and one metre high. He also painted Sir Thomas More. 
The Ambassadors is a very well known painting which shows two bearded dressed in doublet and hose with cloaks edged with ermine and hats, standing either side of a sideboard and table. On the furniture are casually arranges various items, tropes, that represent elements of learning and knowledge which are prerequisite to public life. Some of these are obvious, like the globe and compasses, the bible, others more obscure to us today. In the centre of the painting is a curious trompe l’oeil feature. Invisible to the viewer standing in front of the painting but for a smear like element, viewed from a specified angle a skull can be seen. It is speculated that Holbein merely wanted to display his virtuosity, or that the painting was intended to be hung on a stairway where it would be reliably encountered at the requisite angle. 
Elizabethan artists often specialized in miniature painting. Miniatures were around ten cm high, often oval shaped very detailed head and shoulders portraits. They were used like photographs to carry a likeness of a person around. The miniature had a strategic function too – portraits of possible marriage partners could be conveyed to each party. This was important since marriages between members of the royal family and the nobility were often arranged between different nationalities for political reasons, and the couple would rarely have met before the marriage.
During the Elizabethan period, Europe underwent great political upheavals as the Protestant and Catholic churches vied for power. Alliances were forged and broken and the borders of modern Europe were drawn. By the seventeenth century Europe had entered a phase of prosperity. Amsterdam was the centre of the trade in goods from overseas and became a very wealth city. Artists were employed to represent and display the wealth of the new merchant class.
Venice was also a wealthy and powerful centre of trade, and it was painted many times by Canaletto.
!Canaletto.
Canaletto is world famous for his scenes of Venice in panoramic format showing the grand canal and its palazzos with water traffic passing, painted in very accurate detail and bathed in a golden sunlight. Canaletto also spent some time in London and painted the Thames river front, including the Royal Naval College at Greenwich and St Paul’s Cathedral. Canaletto's painting are among the most frequently stolen in the world.
The influence of Italian classical sculpture spread and statuary was an essential element in architectural design, interiors and gardens. There were many popular sculptors.
!Canova. 
An Italian classical sculptor who made a work called The Three Graces a 2 metre high white marble sculpture of three women in flowing robes holding hands dancing in a circle. This sculpture has been stolen once.

!!DUTCH PAINTING
Dutch seventeenth century painting depicted domestic interiors inhabited by the merchant class. This was the first time people other than the ruling classes had been able to commission artists, because of the new found wealth brought by the import and export business. Painters such as Van Dyck, Rembrandt and Vermeer were not presenting images of authority and power, they were depicting the calm serenity of the domestic sphere, a feminine world inhabited temporarily by men. The paintings reproduced the architecture of the time and some artists such as Saenredam made this their subject. 
! Pieter De Hooch. 
De Hooch painted the life of the Amsterdam merchant class in intimate detail. He was painting at a time when this newly wealthy class wanted to display its wealth to the world, but women were still largely confined to the home. So windows were always open, light streaming in to spaces otherwise candlelit, food was plentiful even fruit (although evidently a luxury). 
De Hooch’s paintings portrayed a range of subjects for example in one a toddling child reaches up to the mound of apples on a woman’s lap. The woman is sitting on a stool on the stone flagged floor and there is a door leading into a room with a window in it behind her. The child is rosy cheeked and blonde. Another shows a back yard with red bricks laid and plants growing along a wall. The architecture of the houses, the brickwork, the construction of doors and windows, the flooring and soft furnishings are faithfully reproduced. 

! Johannes Vermeer.
Vermeer is famous for his painting The Card Players, which shows a group of people sitting round a table at night, the scene lit by lamp and candlelight. The light glows on the faces of the card players but this is a very dark painting. One of the men in the painting stares out at the viewer. 
Vermeer painted numerous self-portraits showing himself both as a young an old man, but like de Hooch he is best known for his paintings of domestic interiors, for example Woman at a Window and another painting of a woman playing the harpsichord. His interiors usually show a room lit from one side by a window, with a door in an opposite wall. The furnishings are comfortable, the floor is often back and white stone tiles, and the walls panelled. If a man is present he is placed by or looking out of the window. The woman is often seated, and looking at the viewer or into the room although the woman playing the harpsichord is in profile. Vermeer wanted to symbolise the different realms inhabited by men and women in the seventeenth century, the outside world and the domestic space. In Woman at a Window a map hangs on the wall behind the woman, as if to amplify this difference.
 Woman with A Pearl Necklace is a very well known portrait of a young woman wearing a simple yellow and blue dress with fabric wrapped round her head and a peal necklace at her throat.
!Rembrandt van Rijn.
Rembrandt is acknowledged to be one of the greatest artists of all time. His paintings are celebrated for the treatment of light in his paintings and for his psychological approach. He tried to show the personality and mood of the sitter, giving the viewer the opportunity to make an assessment about the kind of person they are looking at. Rembrandt used light to great effect, not just in the way he used painting to represent it but also in his composition, in where he place figures in relation to light sources. 
One of Rembrandts best-known works is The Night Watch a painting of a group of people sitting in the dark with candlelight. Another is Girl at a Window which shows a young girl seated at table looking up to a window through which light is streaming into the very dark interior. As with most of Rembrandt’s work, light is the subject of this painting.

!Saenredam.
Saenredam is noted for his church interiors which are generally quite small. One shows the architecture of the Osterkerk in Amsterdam, with tall stone columns, a floor of black and white squares of marble, and people dwarfed by the height of the building. The composition is deceptively ordinary, apparently showing an everyday view of an interior, but the treatment of light in the painting is skilful producing an effect of great serenity. The predominantly black and white painting has its focal point in the black diamond shaped board hanging from one of the columns, concentrating the viewer’s eye on the pattern of black and white, veridical lines and perspective planes in the composition.

During the seventeenth century Europe saw much political upheaval and many significant events took place. Artists recorded these events and began to take on a new role as commentators. 
!!HISTORY PAINTING.
!Delacroix.
The iconic image of the revolution is Delacroix’s Liberty at the Barricades. This shows Liberty, the female symbol of France, astride the barricades, a crowd swirling around her, and tricolour flags waving. Delacroix is one of the first artists to represent current events and his work prefigured the realism of the nineteenth century. 

The French Revolution was a rich source of inspiration for artists and one of the chief exponents was David.
! Jacques Louis David.
David made one of the first topical political paintings, The Death of Marat. The painting was shown at the time it was made and was a direct interpretation of a recent and controversial event. It was also an innovative composition, showing Marat, dead in his bath by his own hand, the knife with which he had stabbed himself on the floor near to his hand. Only Marat’s head and shoulders and arm is visible, his arm trailing over the side of the stone bath which carved from a single block on a pedestal base. The dead man wears a white cloth wound like a turban around his head which lolls on the side of the bath. In his other hand he clutches a book or a letter. His skin is looks cold and blue. Unusually the date is inscribed the corner of the painting, drawing the viewers attention to the fact that it was recent history. 

! Theodore Gericault.
Another French artist who made topical history paintings Gericault is known best for The Raft of the Medusa, a very large (2 metre high) painting which shows a shipwreck scene with the survivors clinging to a raft in a stormy sea. This is a depiction of a real recent event in which sense it was a news picture, conveying very vividly the terror of the survivors so that people could imagine being there.

! Francisco Goya.
One of the greatest painters in history, Goya is well known for his political paintings - including The Disasters of War, The Execution of Maximilian (1814) and a series of works known as The Black Paintings. His paintings are mostly small, around 150 cm high at most.
Goya’s intention was to reveal to a wider public the cruelty of war and the harshness of life for ordinary people. He was also an innovator in terms of his use of paint and colour. The Execution of Maximilian (an emperor) shows a blindfold man on the right of the canvas facing a firing squad on the right side. The scene takes place in front of a hill with some buildings behind it. Smoke is coming from the soldiers rifles. The painting depicts the actual moment of execution and was made very soon after the event. Thus it is news reportage, depicting the deposition and execution of a ruling monarch.
The Disasters of War are a series of around 30 etchings (about 40 cm high) showing scenes of carnage from the battlefield. Soldiers lie dismembered and disembowelled on the ground, they hang from trees, decapitated or genitally mutilated. Goya intended to show the gross cruelty of war and because he chose to use etching his work reached a wide audience who might otherwise have been able to try to ignore the black paintings show the life of ordinary Spanish people eking out a subsistence living on the land, living in one room. The paintings are dark and inky, there is no light. They show the harshness and ugliness of life. There is one in the series that shows a dog in quicksand, an ochre sand hill filling most of the canvas with the dogs head sticking out of it.
Eighteenth century Britain was a rural economy ruled by landed gentry. 

!Thomas Gainsborough.
One of the best known eighteenth century British artists, Gainsborough, made a painting showing a couple and their children with a hunting dog under a tree with a house in the background. The man wears breeches, a tail coat and a hat, and is leaning on a shotgun, the woman has on a fine low necked dress which is light blue and white with a straw hat. They are dressed in their country clothes, but there is no pretence - this is a painting intended to display the couples wealth and social status, showing them confidently engaging the viewer from the rural beauty of their large estate. Gainsborough in fact had a very important painting, a portrait of the new English bourgeoisie, the landed gentry. 

!Joshua Reynolds.
Another English painter of the landed gentry, Reynolds made portraits and landscapes. English landscape painting was particularly important during a period when land ownership was the key to wealth. People commissioned paintings to show how much land they owned.
!George Stubbs.
Stubbs' name is synonymous with paintings of horses, depicted standing side on, on a plain background which are extremely realistically painted.
Eighteenth century life was represented in other aspects by Rubens and Hogarth 
!Peter Paul Rubens. 
Rubens is known for his paintings of buxom rosy cheeked naked women. Rubens showed groups of women relaxing together often out of doors, naked or semi-clothed. Their flesh is almost expressionistically painted and close to the range of colours used to paint the skin tones can clearly be seen. Rubens draws on the classical nude as inspiration but his nudes are ordinary women.

! William Hogarth.
From Hogarth, a British artist and political commentator and satirist, we have a comprehensive documentation of eighteenth century London life in his highly detailed drawings and engravings. His engravings of Bedlam, the notorious London hospital for the insane are famous for their accurate depiction of a horrific place which few people had seen. Hogarth showed the seamy side of life, the injustices and inequities, but contrasted it with images of debauchery, gluttony and indulgence.
!Franz Hals.
Dutch Eighteenth century painter notably of portraits, Hals painting The Laughing Cavalier, a portrait of a man in a large hat with a feather is instantly recognisable.

!William Blake.
English poet and artist known for his visionary drawings and for his illustrated poetry books the Songs of Innocence and the Songs Of Experience. Made a drawing with watercolour of God measuring the world with a pair of compasses which has been much reproduced Dutch seventeenth century painter

 



In the eighteenth century in Europe and America neo-classicism and the study of Greek sculpture was a central part of any artists education. The highest accolades were given to those who could best emulate, interpret and appropriate classical Greek art, taking Greek literature as its narrative theme.
Artists were expected to become skilful at drawing in a realistic style, and to be able to draw from life, model and carve the human form with accuracy. But by the nineteenth century romanticism was beginning to take a hold and landscape and nature were idealised.

! Anton Watteau.
A French artist working in the nineteenth century Watteau made paintings of people in landscapes, often with classical allusions – as was the fashion of the day the aristocracy was fond of acting the peasant (Marie Antoinette, for example) or dressing as mythological characters. The paintings often portrait commissions. Watteau was particularly good at painting trees - at the time trees in paintings were rendered in much the same way by many artists, often by assistants rather than by the artist. Watteau drew and painted virtually every leaf making the trees in his work very realistic and quite distinctive.

!Fragonard.
Fragonard is known for his paintings of women and children in the countryside or in a garden. Fragonard’s many portraits depict an idyllic life, as in Girl on a Swing and his many paintings of society women and children in beautiful dresses. Fragonard’s paintings are full of trees, flowers and grass in the background, and the detail of the subjects clothes is given great attention. They are painted in a soft, feathery delicate style.
!Arnold Bocklin.
During the 1960s Bocklin painted a romantic landscapes idyllic pastorals, shepherdesses and swains in ribboned clothes with the occasional piece of statuary. Like Watteau he painted trees in great detail.
!Boucher.
Boucher also painted romantic landscapes.

!!THE SUBLIME.
The worship of nature found its culmination in the German concept of the sublime, which described nature as vast and never ending, true beauty in which man is almost an irrelevance. The chief exponent of this was Friedrich. 
!Casper David Friedrich.
German nineteenth century romantic painter many of whose works were painted from nature on an island in the Baltic sea off the coast of Germany. And who was chiefly known for his interpretation of the Romantic sublime. Friedrich tried to create a rendering of nature so beautiful as to seem almost like heaven. This deeply subjective approach was of great interest to many critics and writers struggling with the idea of authorship, subjectivity and the purpose of art after Kant. The sublime is also dealt with by Mary Shelley in Frankenstein and by English poets such as Wordsworth and Shelley.
Friedrich's paintings usually less than 200 by 100 cm in size. They nearly always feature a solitary figure in a landscape, for example a moonlit night with a ruined folly on a hilltop peak and a figure on a closer peak the canvas rendered in blues and greys, or a figure in a rain and windswept landscape with barren trees, or a figure in a moonlit garden with a balustrade, a classical statue and a broken urn. He painted in cool reduced palette. Moonlit scenes are rendered in shades of blue. Friedrich's idealism and his pitting of man against the elements made him seem reactionary at times.
! John Martin.
An English artist working in the late nineteenth century Martin painted a kind of inversion of the idea of the sublime. His visionary scenes depicted humankind struggling with the natural world. An example is a painting of a towering dark black and brown mountain at night in which a huge chasm opened up, a volcanic eruption spewing out flames sparks and lava into the darkness. Dwarfed by this landscape lots of people are struggling and fleeing from the lava flow and fires.

English artists did not engage with the sublime but focused on the rural landscape around them and its inhabitants. 
!John Constable.
Constable was an eighteenth century artist who made one of the most famous English paintings, The Haywain, which shows a country scene in which a cart loaded with hay is travelling down a lane. The cart and the farm workers that it carries is very small in relation to the picture as a whole. The painting consists mostly of trees and bushes, predominantly green brown and ochre coloured with a blue sky. It is a peaceful image of a summer day at harvest time. Flatford Mill is almost as well-known, this shows a house on a millpond by a river, trees and vegetation predominate. Constable’s paintings convey specific weather conditions and the time of day very well. They seem to represent an idealised and valued vision of the countryside as a peaceful haven. 
Constable always added an element of red to each painting, a tiny dot or brushstroke of red paint that served to draw the viewer’s eye in to the part of the painting that he wanted them to focus on. It might be the roof of a distant cottage or as in the Haywain, a red garment on a person on the cart.

Other British painters such as Wilson Steer, Joseph Wright of Derby, John Everett Millais and Samuel Palmer explored both landscape and portraiture during the nineteenth century. In the second half of the century the industrial revolution changed life in Europe dramatically and the rural idyll was no longer given centre stage. Although critics such as John Ruskin held up the values of traditional arts and crafts skills, and the study of classical art, artists on the whole discovered new challenges.
!Joseph Mallord Turner. 
One of the best-known British artist, often described as a British impressionist, Turner made semi abstract watercolours and oil paintings of the river Thames and London. He was most interested in depicting weather and light conditions as opposed to landscape or architectural detail. Turner’s paintings are often brightly coloured and made with blurred brushy strokes. He had made one of the river Thames with a rainbow and one of a cloudy sky painted in white and grey. Whistler was very critical of Turner, describing his work as mere daubing.
!James McNeil Whistler.
A painting of fireworks over the river Thames was said by Whistler’s rival Turner to look as if someone had thrown a pot of paint at the canvas. Whistler made many partings of London, but he also had a house in the Lake District in the north of England and many of his landscapes are painted there, as well as domestic interiors and portraits. 
As well as being a painter, Whistler painted murals, for example one at Plas Newydd, the home of the Marquis of Anglesey, in Wales. He prided himself in being able to paint very fast. 
During the nineteenth century the styles of past historical periods were being appropriated by contemporary artists. Classicism realism and romanticism were broad spectrums that included artist working in diverse ways. Some movements were more specific in their aims and distinct in their visual output. 
!!THE PRE-RAPHAELITES.
The Pre-Raphaelites were a group of artists working in England who were inspired by poetry and medieval history. They painted people dressed in clothes inspired by medieval costume but in contemporary fashion, women and men characterised by their full lips and long flowing hair worn parted in the centre and loose. The style of painting was exact and detailed, everything rendered with great realism, the intention being to convey ideal beauty and sentiment. Often the paintings had a decorative border or architectural elements. Pre-Raphaelite painting and sculpture was highly stylised and extremely popular.
! Dante Gabriel Rossetti.
Rossetti was a leading figure in the pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, the society formed by artists and writers interested in working together. These artist s produced paintings that looked to medieval art and legend for their inspiration. Rossetti’s Lady of Shallot is a world famous painting of a drowned woman, the lover of King Arthur, floating in a stream. She is face upwards and looks as if she is merely asleep, wearing a long green dress with her hair floating in a cloud around her head and her hands clasped on her breast. Flowers float in the water around her. 
!Ford Maddox Brown. 
One of the British pre-Raphaelite painters, Maddox Brown made less stylised paintings than Rossetti, for example a painting of a goat in a barren landscape light by a lurid setting sun, painted in orange, purple and green.
The poetic aesthetic extended to other artists.
!Henry Wallis.
A British nineteenth century painter who is best known for his painting of Henry Chatterton, a boy poet who committed suicide. The boy is shown on a bed with a view of a garden through a window behind him, wearing a white shirt which is loose.
!Gustav Klimt.
An Austrian artist working at the turn of century based in Vienna, Klimt painted highly decorative images of women with particular attention to the reproduction of the patterns on textiles and clothes the models are wearing. He also painted landscapes, giving the same attention to the ‘pattern’ of leaves and flowers, such as in his series The Four Seasons, paintings of woodlands and gardens in seasonal colours. Klimt had a very distinctive style of drawing the figure, giving his almost exclusively female subjects a kind of languid beauty. They are often draped in decadent poses or staring straight at the viewer giving an impression of forthrightness, and unabashed hedonism. One example is a painting of a standing woman, her head almost resting on one shoulder, wearing an extraordinarily ornate dress and cloak which has a pattern of multicoloured and gold squares. In fact Klimt's trademark was his use of tooled and flat gold leaf applied to the patterns on fabric in his paintings. He also tended to delineate the shape of the body by the outline of the clothes, so that the figure often appeared to be a solid block. These attributes give Klimt’s work a graphic effect. Another example is a painting of four women who are lying together on a bed. Klimt’s paintings often show women together in intimacy, lying together in an embrace. In this painting the four are intertwined and seen in a kind of false perspective as if from above. It is hardly possible to tell which limbs belong to which head. The fabrics of their clothes are mixed together too so that the composition is a swirl of heads, limbs and pattern, creating a kind of round shape within the frame of the canvas. 
Portrait painters were in great demand in a new social milieu ushered in by the industrial revolution and its resulting new wealth
!John Singer Sargent.
Sargent's paintings show fashionably dressed society figures, the fabric of their clothes very realistically painted, skin glowing and hair shining.
!Berthe Morisot.
French artist Morisot is best known for her portraits and drawings.
!Gwen John.
John's portraits are subtle and she used a sombre palette.
!!ORIENTALISM
Art from Japan, China and the far east, both traditional and contemporary began to appear in Europe and attracted a great deal of attention. 
!Hokusai.
A Japanese eighteenth century artist who is best known for his painting The Wave, was a master of the woodcut and pen and ink drawing and painting. The towering wave in this work is about to crash down on the surrounding sea, representing the power of nature. Hokusai worked in traditional style but his subject matter was innovative. The Wave has become an instantly recognisable image.
!Utamaro. 
Utamaro’s series of hand coloured woodcuts, the work for which he is best known, depicts a series of explicit sexual acts between men and geishas dressed in elaborate kimonos. The women and men perform a range of often gymnastic sex acts and could not be shown in public since they were considered indecent. These works are unusual at the time for their representation of enthusiastic and playful sexuality. 

Overt sexuality was not a subject of art in the eighteenth century. Religious beliefs in Europe would not tolerate the depiction of explicit sexual acts and Victorian Britain did not even permit allusive or veiled reference to sex. A number of artists nevertheless made work on the subject, notably Beardsley.

!Beardsley Aubrey.
Beardsley was a graphic artist whose finely executed and intricate art nouveau style has become instantly recognisable. Beardsley illustrated Oscar Wilde's book of the tale of Salome, and made a large number of explicit sexual images. Influenced by art of China and Japan as were many at the time, Beardsley's characteristic flat highly decorative black and white pen and ink drawings made use of patterns on textiles and clothing of his subjects to great effect, and he was an extremely skilled draughtsman.

As well as an interest in sexuality, many artists were interested in psychology and influenced by contemporary developments in psychology, and other therapeutic and diagnostic methods of the day such as phrenology. Sigmund Freud was beginning his research into the unconscious which was to have a profound influence on artists in the late nineteenth and early twentieth century.
!Messerschmidt.
Messerschmidt was a German artist who in the 1890s made a series of small modelled heads cast in bronze, about 10cms across, a male head with a number of different expressions, often the features are contorted. The heads are titled by the mood or emotion the face is displaying. Messerschmidt was influenced by contemporary developments in psychology, and other therapeutic and diagnostic methods of the day.

!!REALISM.
French realism profoundly influenced European art. Painters like Courbet, Corot, Greuze and Daumier began to try to represent landscape and the figure as objectively as possible.
!Gustav Courbet.
The most influential of the early realists is Courbet who is often referred to as the father of realism. Both Manet and Cézanne acknowledged his influence.
!Corot.
A contemporary of Courbet, Corot is known for his still calm scenes of the French countryside, often featuring roads and tracks and always trees, which he was particularly good at painting and some notable snow scenes. Corot’s paintings are small, no more than 60 cm high.
!Camille Pissarro.
A French landscape painter who spent some time in London painting the river Thames. Pissarro like the cool grey light of London.
!Ingres.
Ingres’ painting of a seated nude is known all over the world. It is a simple, natural and intimate portrait of a woman seated on a bed with her back to the viewer, a white sheet draped round her hips, and a red patterned scarf wound round her head, with her face just visible in profile. It has been remarked that that drawing of the figure is anatomically inaccurate and that if the woman were to stand up her body would be very out of proportion. 

!Renoir Auguste.
Children and women are Renoir’s favourite subjects. His paintings are light bright celebrations of youth, beauty, the countryside, gardens and flowers. He painted himself often, the best-known self-portrait is one in which he is wearing a white painter’s smock and a brown wide brimmed hat with glasses. Renoir’s nudes are fleshy and sensual, the paint applied to create a fuzzy, slightly out of focus effect. A particularly well-known painting shows a little girl with very blonde hair in a dark blue dress standing in long grass and poppies. 
!Caillebotte Gustave.
Caillebotte's landscapes and people are characterised by a richness in the way the paint is applied. A painting of men scraping and polishing a wooden floor is a well known example of his work. The men, wearing dark trousers and white shirts, some wearing caps, are on their knees in a horizontal line across the canvas, working, and the floor has a bright shine on it, created by the daylight streaming through the windows at the back of the room. The painting is mainly dark brown and wood colour, with the windows and the shine on the floor painted in a bluish white.
!Chavannes Puvis de.
French painter of the early nineteenth century who made a painting of a thin young man dark hair and eyes who standing in a small boat on a lake. The man is meant to make us think of Christ.
!!THE INVENTION OF PHOTOGRAPHY.
The realist painters were working just before photography. There was still no way of picturing the world other than to paint it. When photography was invented it had an immediate effect on painting because of the way a photograph defined a definite edge to an image. In a painting the edge was always mutable and subjective. Manet and Degas began to compose their paintings in a different way, framing what they painted in a way that was closer to photography. 
!Manet Edouard.
The French artist who is often described as the father of impressionism, Manet painted in a realistic way but used paint expressively. Manet took great interest in the way a photograph cropped a scene and noted the difference for a painter, who can choose what to include and where. Manet’s work began to have a new look which was at first shocking but immediately influential. Manet was prominent member of the community of artists in Paris and was friends with Courbet, Degas, and Monet. 
Manet painted two very well known works, Olympia, and Dejeuner Sur L’Herbe, as well as Bonjour Monsieur Courbet and the Bar at the Folies Bergere. This last is a very original painting which depicts a bar at the Folies Bergere night club, visited and painted by many of his contemporaries, including Toulouse Lautrec, behind which is a woman serving. On closer inspection what the viewer sees is actually the reflection in the mirror behind the bar. Manet has taken this mirror as the edge of his painting and thus we see the woman’s back, some of people at the bar and the bottles which are in front of the mirror. What was striking at the time was that this was a painting of a reflection, and was not what it seemed to be at all.
Manet's paintings were startlingly new and inventive. His Olympia (which owes much to Velasquez’s Rokeby Venus) was considered shocking, provoked storms of protests and was rejected from the Paris salon, the official body of recognition for artists. The painting was said to be pornographic and the public was outraged. Olympia is depicted nude, reclining on a couch, with her maid and a cat behind her. She wears a ribbon choker and earrings and seems to be a prostitute. 
However it was the look on the woman’s face that was controversial – she stares out of the canvas at the viewer, actively acknowledging their presence and thus making them a subject of the work. This was revolutionary, and brought the whole history of the depiction of the female nude in art into question – here a woman meets the viewer’s gaze rather than allowing the viewer unchallenged access to her body in passive acquiescence.
Dejeuner Sur L’Herbe, also a reworking of a historical painting, provoked a similar response but soon these two paintings were acknowledged as groundbreaking works. Dejeuner Sur L’Herbe shows a group of young men and women picnicking in the woods, one of the women is naked, reclining casually and staring out at the viewer from the centre of the canvas. The gaze of some of the others is also focused out of the picture plane at the viewer.
Bonjour Monsieur Courbet is a painting in which Manet acknowledges his debt to Courbet. He shows himself walking along a country lane, respectfully greeting the passing Courbet who carries a walking stick and wears a hat. 
!Edgar Degas.
Degas is a contemporary of Manet’s who also made use of the photograph in his compositions. Degas is known all over the world for his scenes of ballet dancers rehearsing, which are instantly recognisable and often reproduced in particular as calendars. His paintings are cropped – like a photograph a figure will be half painted cut off by the edge of the canvas. He mainly painted women, including several paintings and drawings of women bathing. One of these shows the back of woman with red hair as she crouches in a tin bath. There is a blue towel next to her on the floor. 
Degas made many bronze sculptures of ballet dancers, most quite small around 50 cm high, as well as some of curled up dogs. His best known and very popular sculpture The Little Dancer is larger, around two metres high. The little girl is realistically modelled and wears a net tutu a bodice and ballet shoes. It is rare to find a bronze sculpture which has added clothes.

!Paul Cézanne.
Known as the father of modernist painting, Cézanne was a French painter working in the mid-nineteenth century. He took the ideas of Manet and Courbet and developed a completely new style of painting. Many see Cézanne as the pivotal figure in late nineteenth century painting who laid the ground for Cubism. Cézanne lived in Paris and the south of France in a house called Jas de Buffon in Provence. The Provencal countryside featured in much of his work and Cézanne liked to work out of doors as much as he could. He also painted still lives and portraits. 
Cézanne was working around the same time as the impressionists, but his ideas took him on a very different route. He had no interest in creating an impression, in depicting light, in optical fragmentation of the image. Rather he was concerned with how the three dimensional world could be rendered on a two dimensional plane. 
Cézanne was one of the first artists to paint a representative image giving as much importance to the picture plane as to the illusory space of the painting. In his landscapes and still lives he considered how much of an object he could paint, seeing no reason not to paint the parts of an object that could not be seen, to bend perspective, to remake nature. He composed his paintings so that the picture plane seemed to come towards the viewer, using judicious touches of white to control the path of the viewers eye. It is in this that he was revolutionary and laid the ground for Picasso and Braque to develop Cubism, for as young artists they were astonished by what Cézanne had done. He gave them the idea that a three dimensional object could be rendered in two dimensions not by creating an illusion, but by unfolding its various planes and presenting them with no relation to the actual physical integrity of the object. 
Cézanne was also daring in his use of colour. He never covered the ground of the canvas completely, always leaving parts of it bare white to function as a part of the painting as a whole. He applied colour with an abstract sensibility, for its own sake as part of the painting as a whole, rather than as a part of a faithful depiction. He used the juxtaposition of pure colours to create shadows of other colours.
A number of paintings are immediately associated with Cézanne. The Bathers (a number of works bear this title) are a group of female nudes in a variety of poses, their bodies represented as planes in a landscape. The painting is brown and green the bodies flesh coloured. This is Cézannes best known and least understood work, and it is the work which has the most influence on other artists. His still lives of oranges and apples are also characteristic, as are some of his portraits, for example the woman with a green face (the green representing the shadow cast on her face by the hat she was wearing. Most of his paintings are fairly small, under a metre high.
He painted the land around his country home endlessly, often making repeated versions of the same view of trees and hillside. He made a small landscape which includes a rectangular water cistern in which there is a mirror image, many paintings of trees including an small painting of a single almond tree in blossom, and views from the house. He often painted his wife, including a notable painting of her in the bath under greenish water.

!!IMPRESSIONISM.
Whilst Cézanne was investigated the depiction of three dimensional space, a number of younger artists who were influenced by photography were experimenting with a new way of painting. The photograph made realistic and accurate representation by artists redundant, since photograph could do this quicker and more accurately. This fact, combined with a new acknowledgment of the subjectivity of art and an interest in the psyche freed artists to try to make impressions of what they saw rather than try to replicate. Broadly speaking, the artists role became one of subjective interpretation rather than of objective representation. The most dramatic example of this is the work of a number of painters working in France in the 1880s and 90s.
!Claude Monet.
Monet is the best known of the Impressionists, and indeed made the painting which gave the movement its name, Impression: Sunrise, a view of the houses of parliament and the river Thames in London. This is a small painting of the river at sunrise, the water reflecting the oranges and pinks of the sun, which is painted as a small orange circle of relatively thick paint. This painting was remarkable for its vagueness in terms of detail, and for the consideration of the nature of the painted surface, so that the sun could be read both as a sun and as a painted circle. 
Monet was trying to paint an impression of what he saw, as if with eyes half shut, and it is possible that he got this idea through seeing out of focus photographic mages. After all the idea of a deliberately blurred image was a radically new one at the time and Monet’s idea changed the aims of painters everywhere. Monet took from Cézanne the idea that an artist might paint what was in front of him but paint less that he actually saw, aiming to reproduce the essence of the view. To this end he developed a very distinctive painting style breaking the scene he was painting into patches of colour, applied without covering the ground of the canvas, using the uncovered white ground to create a shimmering effect. He used colour to paint light as well as solid forms.
Along with Matisse and Picasso, on whom he was influential, Monet is considered by many to be one of the greatest artists in the world and one of the founders of modernism and twentieth century painting
 Monet made several important groups of works. The Water Lily paintings that he made at his country home in Giverny, a series of façades of Rouen cathedral, and a series of Haystacks.  
The water lilies series are largely 3 m by 4 m semi abstract canvases which depict a pond or lake with water lilies on it using shades of emerald, ultramarine and violet, with bright pink and red highlights on the usually white lilies. The way the paint is applied to construct the image is almost of as much importance as the image itself.
A series of paintings of the façade of the cathedral at Rouen was a detailed exercise in and demonstration of impressionist technique. In these works the surface of the painting is encrusted with multicoloured paint, which is visible as a façade only at a distance. There are an immense variety of paintings which represent and exaggerated different light conditions. Monet was unique in his preference for continual repetition of views of the same subject. He also painted hay stacks series, notable for their pale blue and lilac shadows, a houses of parliament and Thames series,
Although best known for these series of large scale paintings, Monet made a large number of one off works preceding these. 

Impressionism did not impress the Paris salon, leading to the formation of the Salon des Refusés, a place for all those artists rejected by the Salon to exhibit. It soon became recognised that these rejected artists were changing painting forever. 
!Georges Seurat.
Known as the inventor of pointillism, Seurat made a number of large paintings in this style, the best known of which is L’Ile de la Grande Jatte, as well as landscapes and coastal views. Pointillism is a way of painting using only tiny dots of separate colour. These dots merge at a certain distance to create an optical illusion of solid colour although there is a grainy quality to the painted image. L’Ile de la Grande Jatte shows people enjoying themselves on a summers day on an island. In the foreground is a boy in swimming trunks wearing a red hat. Women and children walk in the gardens and under the trees and a group of picnickers sit on the grass.
!Alfred Sisley.
Sisley was an English painter who spent much time working in Paris. Sisley was the major exponent of the Impressionist style in Britain, painting London and the English countryside.
!Walter Sickert.
Like Sisley, Sickert painted London in am impressionistic style, although he is not an Impressionist. Sacker’s paintings are all made in gloomy palette of grey, ochre and brown, the paint thickly applied, showing scenes of London and portraits. 
Not all French painters worked in the impressionist style. Many artists developed individual ways of working that placed them outside any movement. 
!!SYMBOLISM.
!James Ensor.
Belgian symbolist artist who lived in Ostend where his former home can still be visited, During the 1890s Ensor made a painting of a crowd of  people including several in clowns masks, one white masked person wearing top hat making a particular impression. The painting has a slightly nightmarish effect.
!Odilon Redon.
Belgian symbolist artist who made paintings, drawings and prints. One of his best-known works is a drawing of a black spider.
The Dutch artist Van Gogh was not part of any movement. His style was idiosyncratic and he made his work in relative isolation, not knowing success in his own lifetime.
!Vincent Van Gogh.
Van Gogh came from a poor family and he was supported financially throughout his life by his brother Theo who became an art dealer and bought all of Van Gogh’s early work. Their relationship is well documented by letters. Without Theo, Van Gogh would not have been able to paint and as it was he lived much of the time on very little money.
Van Gogh began by drawing and painting what he saw around him, rural scenes from his home village, showing people working in the fields including the famous charcoal drawing The Potato Eaters, which shows a poor family eating a meagre meal crammed into one room in the semi-darkness. 
Van Gogh moved to his own lodgings in Paris but continued to paint rural life. He also painted his own surroundings, production another seminal work The Artists bedroom, which shows a small bed, a chair with a straw seat, a pair of boots by the bed. The perspective is exaggerated and the drawing chunky. He outlined the shapes of things, developing what was to become his unique style. He also painted his own boots, which amounted to a self portrait.
During his time in Paris and later on in Arles, Van Gogh met many other artists and became particular friends with Paul Gauguin who had a great influence on him. 
Van Gogh began to paint still life, and in particular flowers, influenced by the fashion for oriental decorative arts which had recently begun to arrive in Paris. He even made a painting of a cherry tree which has Japanese characters round its borders.
Van Gogh’s most famous work, also one of the most famous paintings in the world, is Sunflowers, a painting of a number of sunflowers in a vase, the whole done in a palette of ochre, yellow and brown with Van Gogh’s usual thick encrustations of paint and impasto. This work is now priceless. A similar work, Irises, is worth billions.
Van Gogh and his close friend the painter Paul Gauguin rented a house in Arles in the south of France where the light was very good for painting, where they lived and worked, both drinking heavily. Van Gogh made many of his best works here but it was in Arles that his increasing depression began to take its toll. He painted the portraits of many local people, including the school master, the postman, a local prostitute who modelled for him, and notoriously his own self portraits. One of these shows him as a redheaded bearded man with pale green eyes wearing a suit. But one self portrait is notorious for showing the artist with a bandage round his head covering his ear. He is reputed to have cut off his own ear when drunk and sent it to a woman with whom he was angry.
Van Gogh sold only to his brother in his lifetime and his talent was not recognised. This probably contributed to his increasing drinking and ill health. Towards the end of his life he painted a number of landscapes that popular myth supposes presage his death, cornfields with borrdoing dark skies and black crows flying, the birds painted with black paint squeezed straight from the tube. He often used paint in an unmediated way which was unusual at the time, and it was his use of paint that first brought him to attention, although his subject matter on the whole was conventional.
Van Gogh’s life has been mythologized, helped by a film made in the 1960s starring Kirk Douglas, which shows him as the ultimate tortured soul putting his life into his art.
!Paul Gauguin.
Gauguin’s early works were landscapes and portraits. Gauguin was an outspoken person and an influential among the artists of Paris. He was a very close friend of Van Gogh and they lived together in Arles in the south of France for a time. Gauguin supported Van Gogh and encouraged him, helping him through periods of madness including the ear dismembering incident. Gauguins paitnigns are mainly bold and coluourful figurative works.
Towards the end of his life Gauguin moved to Tahiti (where he later died) and painted the works with which he is most readily associated (although they are not necessarily seen as his best), showing Tahitian people, often women, in native dress, with the interiors of traditional homes and beach scenes. The colours he uses are very strong bold and sun drenched. 
!Pierre Bonnard.
Bonnard’s domestic interiors are painted in warm and subtle shades with soft dabbing brushstrokes, and his attention to the pattern and detail of the textiles in the room is an important part of the whole composition. In one painting a woman is seated at a table at the top of the canvas, the table extending towards the bottom, the perspective elongated which is designed to draw the viewer in and give the impression both of welcome and of distance. much of Bonnard’s work presents people in a solitary and restful state.
!Edouard Vuillard.
Like Bonnard, Vuillard painted landscapes and domestic interiors.
!Henri de Toulouse Lautrec.
Toulouse Lautrec was a graphic artist who was friends with many of the artist of fin de siècle Paris. He is best known for his posters for the Folies Bergère in particular for one showing a row of can-can dancers. He was a very good draughtsman and was influence by Chinese and Japanese art.
!Henri Rousseau.
Rousseau’s painting of a tiger in a jungle has become extremely well known. The jungle is shown as a mass of enormous green leaves and stalks that cover the canvas, below which stalks a partially obscured tiger. There are a number of other animals, birds and exotic blooms. The painting is executed in a faux naïve illustrative style. Rousseau also made a painting of a parade in a French town, he often represented French civic life and was a Republican. 
!!FAUVISM.
!Andre Derain.
Another group of French artists led by Andre Derain and influenced by both Cézanne and Monet painted in a style that they called Fauvism, meaning literally ‘wildness’. They worked in very bright pure colours, using larger areas of colour than the impressionists and plenty of white space, not attempting to create a recognisable image but to reduce the image to a wild approximation of its essential qualities.
Although Impressionist painting became very popular, it was the work of Cézanne that inspired the next generation of artists notably Matisse, Picasso, Gris and Braque to break new ground. 
!!CUBISM.
!Georges Braque.
A French artist born around 1880, Braque along with Picasso was the major exponent of Cubism, a new style of painting that fragmented and distorted the image in a radical way. Although he began as a painter of landscapes and portraits, in 1911 Braque made a painting on which he pasted a piece of newspaper, Still Life with a Guitar. It showed a table a bottle and a guitar but treated all objects as if conventional perspective did not apply, representing them instead with flat planes and areas and shapes. The newspaper signified the prescience of a newspaper in the still life, but was not a newspaper and did not look like one.
The act of sticking something on to the canvas initiated a revolution in painting, because it destroyed the integrity of the picture plane by acknowledging its physicality as a surface. This had the effect of allowing a painting to be seen as an object as well as a flat representation. No one had ever stuck anything onto a canvas to make part of the image before. Braque’s achievement was to find a new way of representing form. He began to include collage, using old tickets, printed paper (in one painting he uses paper printed with a rattan pattern to signify a cane chair), in another a piece of wood effect paper for a guitar for example. He made many such collages often depicting bottles, tabletops, wine glasses, with the structural planes of the objects opened out and repositioned, before going on, as did Picasso to attempt the same thing exclusively with paint.
Cubism was a way of representing all the planes of three dimensional objects on a two dimensional plane, to think of every object in terms of the surfaces of a series of cubes for the purposes of painting. This way of looking at the physical world had been initiated by Cézanne, w
Such a radical restructuring of pictorial space resulted in paintings made in browns and greys initially. Braque sometimes used an elliptical canvas. The paintings gave the impression of crystalline forms with recognisable elements attached, such as a head, an eye, part of a guitar, the side of a bottle, the front of a table, the back of a chair. 
Gradually Braque began to make freer images, the effect of cubism had been to free the picture plane from the constraints of realistic representation and pictorial space. The surface of the canvas became the pictorial space, so that the painting was both a representation and an exploration of surface. 
!Juan Gris.
A painter working at the same time and in a similar way to Braque. It is possible that Gris was the first to use collage on a painting, although Braque is given the credit for this. Gris tended to make small paintings, sometimes using an oval format. He also went on to make Cubist paintings.
!Pablo Picasso.
the Spanish artist Picasso is considered to be the greatest modern artist in the world. He was born in the late nineteenth century and made his greatest works in the 1910 and the 1940s..
Picasso’s work progressed through a number of different styles, including Cubism and he made work in a variety of media. His skill at drawing is consistently evident, but he was equally good at sculpture.
From Picasso’s early career, his Blue period is the best known. This describes a number of figurative paintings that have a blue light cast on them, for example a boy with a dove in his hand, and a young boy dressed as a Harlequin balancing on a ball. The dove and the clown, particularly Harlequin and Pierrot were recurring motifs throughout Picasso’s life.
In the early 1900s Picasso, along with Braque and Gris, would have seen African sculptures for the first time when a collection was exhibited in Paris. This, combined with Cézannes experiments with the picture plane, led the three artists to make the first cubist works. 
Although Cubism is most readily attributed to Picasso, it was Gris who in fact made the first Cubist collage. Picasso made a number of these collages featuring bottles, guitars, and also some small sculptures including one of a shelf with some domestic objects on it made from rough bits of wood, painted with a bobble braid tacked along the front. 
Picasso made a great number of Cubist paintings, which are rarely more that a metre high and predominantly brownish or greyish. 
During this period Picasso began to develop his mature style to reach its greatest achievement in two paintings, Guernica and Les Demoiselles D’Avignon. Guernica was painted in 1917 to commemorate General Franco’s massacre of a Spanish village. In it Picasso wanted to show the horror of war and suffering, and he was conscious of making a painting which would get attention. He made the painting immediately after the event and it was intended to be given to the town, although it never was. Guernica is a very large painting, 3 meters by 6, which shows a number of figures based clearly on African sculpture (for example Dogon masks) painted in black, purple, white and grey with everything outlined in black. It is an image of horror, of death and suffering, of weeping women, corpses and maimed bodies. The figures have mask like faces and their limbs are intertwined so that the whole painting operates as an abstract composition as well.
This was the first time a modern artist had made a painting that was such an explicit criticism of the government of the day. Guernica was shocking and reproductions of painting were instantly printed in newspapers all over the world.
Les Demoiselles D’Avignon is composed along similar lines but depicts a more pleasant scene of naked women in the countryside, painted in flesh tones and greens. However the women have the same mask like faces as the figures in Guernica. A film was made of Picasso making this painting, showing how he drew out the figures with continuous lines never once correcting himself or erasing anything.
Picasso painted many portraits, often of the various women in his life, including Dora Maar, one of his lovers. These were never flattering, as the Cubist style which Picasso had made his own led to features being rearranged, an eye might appear on the side of the head and the face be cut in two. Black painted lines delineate everything and colour is entirely unrealistic.
Picasso made a great deal of carved and assembled sculpture including a bull’s head made from a bicycle seat with a pair of handlebars for horns.
Drawing underpinned everything that Picasso did, and he made a large number of them. In particularly a number of black line drawings of the Minotaur, a myth which fascinated him. Later in he made coloured lithographs, which look like pastel drawings, which are very well known, such as a simple hand holding a bunch of flowers and a dove with an olive branch in its beak. 
In his exploration of every medium Picasso included ceramics, producing a great many plates, vases, and other objects, which he painted on and also made a series of tooled and embossed silver platters. 

!Juan Miro.
Picasso’s compatriot Miro took a very different route, making abstract paintings that drew on a variety of sources. Miro uses amorphous shapes reminiscent of microscopic life forms which are painted on a thinly prepared raw linen ground. Thin black lines and spidery nodes connect amorphous shapes in red and blue. Miro borrowed compositional elements from comic strip drawings in particular the American Krazy Kat. He also made collages using images cut from cutlery, tool and machinery catalogues which were often mixed with elements from cartoon strips and then abstracted to produce his paintings. In some ways Miro’s approach was had an affinity with the work of the surrealists
Few artists have come close to achieving Picasso’s huge output in such a variety of media. But one French artist made paintings that were as influential as Picasso’s.
! Henri Matisse.
Matisse is considered one of the greatest artists in the world. His paintings include the famous Dance, an 8 metre by 3 metre panoramic painting of dancing female nudes, radical for its semi abstraction. The pink figures become rhythmic arabesques on the swirling blue ground. Matisse later made the figures into paper cut-outs, a form in which he was prolific in later life, using ultramarine paper collaged onto a white ground. 
With the Snail, a large 4 by 3 metre collage made of a spiral of squares of different brightly coloured pieces of paper within a yellow collaged frame, these cut-outs became Matisse's best known work, their graphic simplicity lending itself well to printed reproduction. The blue dancers, their heavy limbs creating a calligraphic pattern, are one of the most recognisable works by a twentieth century artist.
Matisse also made a large number of drawings; he had an assured hand, creating very simple drawings often using one continuous line. In his paintings it is Matisse’s use of drawing which sets his work apart. He allowed a drawn edge to define the pictorial space and function both as part of an object or structure and part of the composition, and similarly he left areas of the canvas unpainted, allowing the white of the ground to function as part of the image. 
Matisse painted restful, joyous and domestic subjects in an entirely unsentimental, intellectual way. His composition was daring, for example the Piano Lesson is a predominantly green and red painting composed of large blocks of colour, the entire painting bisected by a diagonal line, the head of a boy emerging from behind a piano very small in relation to the very large canvas. The painting is figurative but it verges toward abstraction. 
It is this abstract sensibility and his use of colour which singles Matisse out. A head and shoulders portrait of a woman with a green face shows her with the shadow of her woman’s hat over her face painted in a swoop of emerald green. Matisse made literal translations of what his eye saw, so that if a shadow looked greenish, he painted it green, and if an edge could be made by not painting the canvas, he left it bare.
For example, another painting depicts a goldfish in a bowl on a table by a French window opening onto a tiny balcony. The window and room are boldly outlined in black; the goldfish bowl and much else is half-painted. The Red Studio is a daring work, showing the furniture and objects in his studio outlined in white (made simply by leaving the canvas white where he wanted the line to be) and some colour, with minimal perspective, resulting in a painting that is a red square of canvas with objects, and some architectural elements such as a pair of shutters floating on it. 
Matisse also made a number of bronze casts, the best known of which is the triptych The Back, an abstracted human torso viewed from the back, carved in relief as semi cylindrical shapes to represent the two sides of the back and the buttocks, leaving a space in the middle to represent the division caused by the spine. The sculptures were commissioned for a specific interior and were thus made as three bronze wall reliefs. 
At the turn of the century, the influence of photography on painting was clear. It was also having an effect on sculpture. This period saw some of the greatest sculptors emerge, leaving behind the tradition of copying classical sculpture and measuring from life. 
!!EARLY TWENTIETH CENTURY SCULPTURE.
!Auguste Rodin.
Rodin, a French artist who is considered to be one of the greatest modern sculptors, made his best-known works at the turn of the century. Two of these, The Kiss (1901) and The Thinker are known all over the world.
Rodin’s achievement was to use traditional stone and bronze cast sculpture techniques to represent movement, and to draw attention to the physical material property of the sculpture as a block of stone. Rodin did not want to make sculpture that looked lifelike but was closer to the impressionists and cubists in his desire to acknowledge surface and material as an important part of the work for its own sake. 
Rodin saw the plinth as an integral part of the sculpture to be acknowledged rather than seen as an unfortunate necessity, again drawing attention to the sculpture as an object rather than something which should try to replicate reality. He often left parts of the plinth unfinished or made his figures emerge from the stone of the plinth.
Although Rodin was able to carve and model the human form with great accuracy he often exaggerated proportions to enhance the impression he wanted a particular work to make, using the tricks of classical sculpture such as perspective and foreshortening. This can be seen very clearly in The Kiss, a white marble sculpture which shows a seated male and female nude entwined in a kiss. The figures are about one and a half times larger than life, their bodies are carved without excess of detail, and the feet and legs are exaggerated. The couple grow out of the stone block on which they sit which is left roughly carved. The Kiss has become a world famous representation of romantic and sexual love. Rodin made several copies and a number of smaller bronze versions.
There is a similar bronze of a girl lying on a low plinth, her body at points disappearing into the plinth as though it has not yet been formed. In this work Rodin wanted to express the state of sleep. 
Rodin’s Thinker is an iconic work, a slightly larger than life size seated male nude with one elbow on a knee, the hand made into a fist on which the bent forward head rests. The sculpture is made in bronze, with a very dark almost black patina and the modelling is relatively rough. It grows out of a plinth about one metre high, raising the seated figure to the standing viewer’s eye level. The Thinker is a representation of existential angst, and gained its reputation by succeeding in the three dimensional representation of a state of mind. It is frequently reproduced, used in advertising or merely invoked in conversation by mimicking the physical gesture made by the figure. This quotation of Rodin’s pose is both a reference to the actual sculpture and more generally to illustrate the idea of intense thought
Walking Man is Rodin’s solution to another challenge – how to represent motion in figurative sculpture. This is a life-size dark brown bronze sculpture of a naked striding man stands on the floor on a minimal plinth so that the viewer is on the same level. 
Rodin made public commissions, the best known of which is The Burghers of Calais, which shows an event in French history in which the civic leaders of the town of Calais were imprisoned. Rodin’s sculpture shows them chained together, standing front to back and forming a melancholy circle, which draws the viewer to it. The sculpture itself forms a cage made by the bronze circle of figures.
A statue of the French writer Balzac is a radical departure from contemporary portraiture – Balzac is shown standing, wrapped in a floor length cloak so that only his head is visible. He has wild hair and a fierce expression and the figure leans back at an almost unrealistic angle. 
A commission for a pair of monumental bronze gate for a public building in Paris resulted in The Gates of Hell, a bronze relief that occupied Rodin for years. It is a densely modelled depicting of purgatory covered with figures, for which Rodin did a large number of sketches and studies in bronze.
!Constantin Brancusi.
Another artist who considered the plinth to be a part of the sculpture was Brancusi, who lived and worked in Paris at the end of the 19th century. Brancusi’s works were often no more than elaborated plinth shapes, with simple forms placed on them made in plaster, bronze, wood and marble. One of his best known works is Endless Column, a polished bronze column on a wooden plinth made of truncated three dimensional diamond shapes placed on top of the other, about 10 metres high. He also made a small carved head of a sleeping person which is so smooth and almost featureless as to almost appear to be a large stone.
!Alberto Giacommetti.
In contrast Italian artist Giacommetti had little interest in the plinth. Giacommetti’s bronze sculptures are extremely elongated usually standing figures, sometimes single figures, sometimes pairs or groups which often imply movement, or seem to be about to walk. They are solitary and isolated figures, reduced to essences of the human form. The sculptures are not more than human height and often much smaller, the heads are tiny, the legs and arms no more than rods of metal, the bodies agglomerations of bronze that seen to serve merely to join the limbs, not much more than 30 cm wide. The sculptures are modelled roughly so that the metal looks as if it has been dripped, dragged and pinched. 
Giacommetti made a large number of working drawings. 
!Gaudier- Henri Brzeska.
Gaudier Brzeska made stone and bronze sculptures, mostly small and medium sized, for example a small dark stone carving of a curled up sleeping fawn.

For the first time artists were entirely free to make whatever they wanted, the only constraint being financial. Photography began to be seen as part of an artists practise, abstraction was on the horizon, and the old traditions ceased to hold sway. Several major movements carried art into the twentieth century and the period known as modernism. These are surrealism, futurism and dada.
!!SURREALISM.
Surrealist art was made in the context of the publishing of Freud’s work on the subconscious, and of the liberation of art from straightforward narrative or representation of the world. The subconscious mind offered new worlds to explore.
!Dali Salvador.
This Spanish artist, born in 1900 is with Magritte the most famous of the surrealists and a candidate for best known artist in the world. Dali’s paintings or even details of his paintings are instantly recognisable such as the melted clocks which from a small part of one painting but which are instantly associated with Dali and surrealism. Dali’s surrealism is fantastical, he created dreamlike landscapes filled with distorted objects, nightmarish scenes and apocalyptic wastelands and religious iconography. One painting shows mutant human bones that seem to have a life of their own in a grey green no-mans land, the aftermath of a war. Another shows the crucifixion from Christ’s point of view, showing what he would have seen looking down from the cross, the whole image a luridly coloured hallucination. A landscape with dead trees has clock faces draped on the trees and on the ground apparently melting and dripping. A giant red rose floats in the air above a desert. A telephone receiver is replaced by a lobster. 
Where Dali’s use of obvious symbolism and his lurid hallucinatory images have made him enduringly popular, his personal eccentricity has only increased his popularity and is difficult to separate from his work. Dali dressed as a dandy, often wearing white, and sporting a very long waxed moustache that extended beyond his face and pointed up. He lived with his wife Galia in a house in Spain at Figuera da Foz where the couple were host to many of their contemporaries.
Dali is not considered to be the intellectual force behind surrealism, however well known his work has become. This role is given to Breton.
!Breton Andre.
The most outspoken member and leader of the surrealists in Paris at the beginning of the twentieth century, Breton made paintings and sculpture, and wrote the Surrealist manifesto which set out to define a new kind of art for new times. His best known work is a clothes iron with spikes set into the sole plate. 
!Magritte Rene.
Magritte is famous of the Surrealists, and indeed is one of the best-known artists in the world. A French painter born in 1850, Magritte’s images are instantly recognisable and attributable, and have been much copied. They have influenced advertising imagery for several decades and continue to do so. 
Magritte was interested in both visual and textual puns, for example, ‘Ceci n’est pas une pipe’ – ‘this is not a pipe’ is inscribed on a painting of a pipe. Many of his paintings feature both words and objects often the objects are apparently named but the names do not make sense. In this way Magritte's text paintings are both object and subject in the sense that are what they are but the viewer is then told that they are not, leading them to a new consideration of what they are seeing and of what art might do.
Many of Magritte's paintings are very well known; a train emerging from a fireplace, an apple obscuring most of the front of a bowler hatted man’s face, an inverted mermaid: with a fish’s head and woman’s legs and pelvis, an easel by a window with a painting on it, the painting showing the view through the window, two grey spaceship like objects titled ‘Floating Spheres’, and a man’s face which is also a female torso in which the pubic hair is also the man’s beard. These are painted in a highly realistic but flat and stiff style, with a muted palette, smooth, flat and few brushstrokes. The ludicrous juxtapositions of objects and people are nonetheless poetic and leave the viewer hoping that they might actually happen, that the everyday might mix up and become magical, in other words surreal.
!Man Ray.
A French surrealist sculptor painter and photographer working in Paris in the early twentieth century. Man Ray experimented with photography to create unique images, using various methods including placing objects on light sensitive plates and then exposing them to get an image. Man Ray took a well-known photograph of Duchamp with a star shaved out of the back of his head smoking a pipe. 
! Meret Oppenheim.
Oppenheim made a sculpture of a fur covered cup, saucer and spoon, as well as one of a pair of white high heeled shoes upside-down on a silver salver trussed with string and with paper frills on the ends of the heels. She also made a pair of leather gloves, the conventional stitched ridges on the backs of each made to resemble the veins on the back of a hand.
!Hans Bellmer.
Bellmer made photographs of dolls and parts of dolls arranged in tableaux, as well as sculptures. His work deals with sexual taboo.
!Lazlo Moholy Nagy. 
This Hungarian artist was a contemporary of the surrealists and is best known for a number of pioneering semi-abstract films and photographs using architectural form, light and shade. 
Surrealism provided the context for an avant garde group of artists, performers and writers to produce work that they collectively titled Dada.
!!DADA.
The dada artists were based in Berlin but drawn from a number of different nationalities. They worked in all media and also made performance and musical work, performing regularly under the name Cabaret Voltaire. The name comes is a combination of a French word for hobby horse but was intended to have no specific meaning.
!Marcel Duchamp. 
Known in the twentieth century as the father of conceptualism, the French artist Marcel Duchamp was among the artists escaping Europe for America to avoid censorship or persecution as Jews. 
He founded Dada and became notorious for his controversial usually sculptural works, in particular the ones that became known as ready-mades. The notorious Fountain, exhibited at the Armoury show in New York in 1913 was in fact a gents ceramic urinal placed on its sided so that it could be said to have the shape of a Buddha or a wall fountain. Duchamp signed the urinal on the front, R. Mutt, (one of the invented punning names that Duchamp used to sign his work). 
Fountain created a furore because it was a bought object, to which nothing had been added, that Duchamp claimed to make into a work of art merely by the act of signing it and proclaiming authorship. The radical step taken by Duchamp with his ready-mades, a cycle wheel attached to the seat of a stool for example, or a metal bottle dryer, was to have far-reaching implications for art. 
Duchamp employed pseudonyms and puns as part of his work. He created a female alter ego Rrose Selavy (a pun on ‘C’est la vie’) who authenticated and signed many of his works including the urinal. He photographed himself dressed as Rrose, a rather unconvincing manifestation. Rrose produced a perfume named La Belle Haleine literally ‘beautiful breath’ intended as an allusion to its opposite, bad breath and a pun on ‘La belle Helene’, Helen of troy. 
Inframince or infrathin was a concept involving the declaration of an invisible or intangible and banal act or action to be a work of art. For example the static friction and the sound of velvet trouser legs rubbing together and the warmth left on a recently vacated seat, 
Almost as notorious as Fountain was Duchamp’s defaced reproduction of the Mona Lisa. Making a pun on the letters LHGC which appear below the face of the Mona Lisa he wrote that ‘elle a un grand cou’ which translates as ‘she has a fat arse’ and drew a moustache on her face. This re-appropriated image became one of the best known images in twentieth century art. 
Duchamp made a number of bronze and wax sculptures which were casts of male and female sexual parts, taking, for example, a mould from female genitals which gave him a negative from which he cast in bronze rather than make a positive from it. 
One of Duchamp's last works was L’Origin du Monde, a large peepshow containing a wax female torso, laid on an apparent mossy bank, the head out of view, with the genitals exposed. There is a drawing by Courbet of a nude in a similar pose. The apparently innocent tableau with its celebratory title became due to the voyeuristic way in which the viewer was required to look at it tainted with an atmosphere of illicit pleasure, abuse or even murder. 
The Large Glass, also known as The Bride Stripped Bare by her Bachelors Even is a large sculpture comprising a screen print onto two pieces of glass held in a metal freestanding frame which was made in an edition. This is Duchamp’s least understood work and it has always resisted interpretation. The images printed on the glass are geometric rays, lines, and a coffee grinder with three buckets which was a motif Duchamp repeatedly returned to and had made several drawings of. The buckets on the grinder are sometimes supposed to represent the bachelors, but in general the meaning of the work remains opaque.
Duchamp also made paintings several of the coffee grinder that appears in the Large Glass.
Duchamp was photographed by Man Ray with a star shaved out of the back of his close cut hair. He played chess every day of his life.
!Kurt Schwitters.
Schwitters, a leading Dada artist and poet, invented the concept of Merz, a way of working and making art, that he applied to everything he did. The most significant manifestation of this is the Merzbau, the interior of a house of which he transformed in its entirety by constructing organic and crystalline protrusions and projections from timber, wire, plaster and papier maché. The rooms became like white caves or cubist grottos. Schwitters also made the (unfinished) Merzbarn during the time he spent in the Lake District in England, a small stone cottage which he transformed with cement and plaster reliefs. 
Schwitters began his career as a graphic designer and typographer and produced innovative work using unusual combinations of typefaces. He developed an interest in type as a visual form and much of his later work incorporates type in the form of cut out and collaged letters and drawn text. 
Schwitters wrote poetry, which he performed at Dada performances and at Cabaret Voltaire. His tone poems consisted mainly of sound rather than words the best known is titled Caravane, which Schwitters intoned through a megaphone. He regarded his poems as aural images. 
Schweitzer’s small collages are made from coloured paper, printed paper, cut out printed letters, found images, drawing, paint and other materials. He made a great number of these, mostly postcard sized which just predate Braque’s and Gris’ use of collaged text as part of an image.
Schwitters also made three dimensional objects, reliefs and assemblages from found materials, timber, wire and paint.
!Johannes Baader.
Baader was a German artist involved in Dada and Cabaret Voltaire performances. Baader also worked on his own and carried out a number of extreme public performances, often involving physical risk to himself. He made a lot of pamphlets and distributed them in mass public leafleting actions for which he was frequently arrested. 
!Hugo Ball.
Ball was a leading Dada performance artist and a prominent member of Cabaret Voltaire, instigating many of the collaborative performances and writing many of the poems and scripts for them. 
!Luigi Russolo.
The Art of Noise, Russolo’s orchestra, played at many Cabaret Voltaire performances. Russolo was an experimental musician and composer; there were no conventional instruments in his orchestra, which consisted of household appliances and especially made instruments often producing a deliberate cacophony.
Italy produced an avant garde at the turn of the century which bears comparison with Dada but had entirely different stated aims. There had been no Italian surrealism but de Chirico’s painting presented a reflexive view of Italian culture and civic life.
!Giorgio De Chirico.
The towns and architecture depicted in de Chirico’s work are very distinctive for their deserted atmosphere, as if time has stood still and the buildings cast long shadows in a strong sunny light. One painting of an empty town square, has a statue of an unspecified man, a tower and an arched arcade or cloister. The shadows cast by buildings and statue on the yellow ochre ground and under the arcade are black. Another painting has a the fallen head of a classical marble statue, very like Michelangelo’s David, seen lying on its side on the ground, with a broken off hand in the background, offering a critique of Italy’s reliance on the culture of its past rather than of the present.
!!FUTURISM.
In this environment of stultification and Mussolini’s dictatorship, the Futurists, who were based in Milan, wrote a manifesto declaring that speed was their god and that the Future was the only thing worth making art about. They were reflecting the times, in a period when manufacturing design was changing rapidly, people and news could travel fast and moving pictures were available to entertain everyone. 
!Frederico Marinetti.
The leading member of the Italian Futurist movement, Marinetti was an eccentric didactic who produced many paintings and published texts include several versions of the Futurist Manifesto. He wrote a Futurist Cookbook, in which he declared pasta to be the most important (and futurist) of all food. The book contains many edible recipes including several for pasta, but also some very inedible and often ironic ones involving rose petals and entirely green banquets, for example, with complicated presentation methods. It offers a glimpse into the surprising romanticism of the futuristic movement. 
Marinetti made several performance works including one in which a set of dining chairs were made to dance on and off the stage. 
!Giacomo Balla.
Balla pursued an obsession with speed in his paintings, trying to find the ultimate way to depict people, animals and objects in motion. A portrait of a dachshund walking with spoke like lines emanating from each of its legs is one example. 
!Umberto Boccioni.
Boccioni is considered to be the best futurist painter and sculptor, much of his work dealing with how to represent speed in two or three dimensions. A bronze sculpture about one metre high called simply ‘Speed’ depicts a striding man, with apparently aerodynamic finlike structures representing trails of movement leading backwards from his limbs and the back of his head. The streamlined helmet shape of his head and the armour like fins give the sculpture a sinister warrior like quality.
Boccioni made a head of Mussolini by drawing a profile copied from a photograph and using this as a guide to a piece of wood. The result is a solid object which has the shape of a vase but the unmistakeable profile of a man from whichever angle it is regarded.
He also made many paintings of his native Milan usually with lines radiating to represent movement of people and vehicles. 
!Frances Picabia.
Picabia’s work did not follow Futurist prescription but he did have an interest in technological development. Picabia’s work does not fit into any movement but he was very influential and is best known for paintings that depict human machine like figures, abstract paintings and his use of text. Picabia also worked as a graphic designer and designed typefaces. 
!Tamara de Lempicka.
In France, Lempicka painted the newly liberated women of the time. Lempicka appears in her own work, distinctive for her straight bobbed hair with low fringe and fashionable clothes.
In the early twentieth century many artists made work that depicted a futuristic world, either utopian or dystopian. Some reflected on the increasingly urban, industrialised way of life.
!Fernand Léger.
Léger, a French painter, had a distinctive style, painting figures as if their bodies were composed of tubular elements, in shaded greys on a whiter ground with occasional additions of primary colours. Léger’s figures look like metal puppets or robots.
Other artists concentrated on the traditional forms of portrait and still life.
!Ernesto Modigliani.
The name Modigliani is always associated with a certain type of portrait, typically of a seated woman with an elongated body and stylised face. Modigliani's portraits are contemplative and painted in a sombre palette with black outlines.
!Giorgio Morandi.
Most of Morandi’s still life paintings depict the simplified forms of domestic objects such as jugs, bottles and vases on plain coloured grounds.
!Nicholas De Stael.
De Stael also painted simple still life using flat bright colours.
!!EARLY PHOTOGRAPHY.
The work of American photographers in the first few decades of the twentieth century was very influential. They recorded both the social conditions of the depression and the opening up of the vast landscapes of the west. Among them were Edward Weston and William Egglestone who photographed city life. 
!Walker Evans.
Evans took photographs of rural America during the thirties Depression that showed the extreme poverty and suffering of ordinary people.
!Ansel Adams. 
Adams was a pioneer of landscape photography known chiefly for his images of American national parks and wildernesses, such as Yosemite and Yellowstone. When he made these photographs the wildernesses were not well explored and many parts were remote and inaccessible, necessitating many days trekking with equipment to get the images he wanted. The photographs capture the enormity and grandiosity of the landscape and came to represent the American wilderness to the rest of the world. Because of their huge scope and precise depth of field they seem to be photographic records of time passing although of course they only record a moment in time. An example is a photograph of the central valley in Yosemite which shows a huge waterfall cascading down the opposite side of the valley.
In Europe, photographers like Eugene Atget and Bill Brandt were recording events and Brassai was beginning to make abstract images.
!Andresz Kertesz.
The Eastern European photographer Kertesz made images which became instantly familiar, including one of the Arc de Triomphe in Paris. Seen from a bird’s eye view there is snow on the road surface and the tracks of the few vehicles on the streets have inscribed black parabolas around the arch on the white ground.
!Claude Cahun.
Cahun was a French photographer who made a number of self portraits in which she is dressed as a man without disguising her sexual identity.
!!THE MOVING IMAGE.
Artists were trying to represent movement in different ways. The Futurists wanted to represent speed and Rodin to create a sculpture of the human form that conveyed movement. The invention of moving pictures was less of an early influence on them than the work of one photographer.
! Eadweard Muybridge
At the turn of the century, Muybridge, a American photographer, made a series of photographs of a horse and a man in motion. He took a series of exposures at close intervals, producing an image for every second of movement. The resulting images had a great influence on artists.
Muybridge's sequential exposures showed a specific action, for example the components of the movement of a naked walking man and of a galloping horse. In the days before moving pictures, no one had foreseen that movement could be represented photographically, reduced to a sequence of still images, or been able to study movement. 
Design and architecture were changing rapidly in Europe and America, as a new style emerged, known as International Modernism. This was a sleek, unadorned way of building championed by Le Corbusier among others. Modernist architects were, like the futurists, enthusiastic about the future, proclaiming that in the future everybody would have a home of their own, a modular unit or machine for living in. Design schools were established to train a new breed of architects and designers. The Bauhaus and De Stijl are the best known. 
!!THE BAUHAUS.
The Bauhaus was in Berlin and was a multi-disciplinary school with a distinct ethos. Students studied across all areas, including theatre design, textiles, ceramics, and graphics as well as architecture and industrial design. Some Bauhaus students went on to become artists.
!Oskar Schlemmer.
Schlemmer studied at the Bauhaus as a theatre designer and he made a number of sculptural designs for the Bauhaus productions, later becoming an artist.
De Stijl was the Dutch design school at which Gerrit Reitveld, the designer of the famous Red Blue Yellow chair, studied. The influence of the painter Mondrian and Russian constructivism can clearly be seen in De Stijl design.
!Piet Mondrian. 
Mondrian was a Dutch artist who began by painting his local countryside, trees and canals before abstracting these scenes and simplifying his palette. Mondrian also experimented with canvas shape, making a series of abstract works on oval canvases that were based on trees in a snowy landscape. These were conveyed as geometric grey and brown marks on a white ground and Cézanne was influenced by them.
Mondrian made a series of works on small square canvases painted on a white ground, dividing the canvas into irregular blocks with black horizontal and vertical lines and colouring in some of the resulting squares and rectangles with red, yellow, blue. These works are among the most famous abstract paintings in the world.
Later Mondrian began to paint patterns using smaller units and areas, for example ‘Who’s Afraid of Red, Yellow and Blue?’ 1910 and one titled Subway, which are a homage to the architecture and the new jazz scene of New York. Mondrian's abstract geometric compositions owe something to contemporary Russian constructivists but are significant for their abstraction of landscape into pure geometric form.
!!CONSTRUCTIVISM.
In Russia, artists began to make paintings and sculpture that was based on architectural form. This was a reaction to the prevailing socialist realist art sponsored by the state, which often amounted to little more than soviet propaganda. Art served the state by representing rural agricultural life or as idyllic but productive, and portrayed the worker as the hero of the day. Artists rebelled by finding a way of working that was entirely different, and most importantly, abstract. Vladimir Tatlin made his spiral staircase monument. Constructivism used geometry, sharp edged straight lines and primary colours and the artists laid down the principles on which works could be made. It had a great influence on contemporary graphic design and typography, and continued to do so throughout the twentieth century.
Constructivism was later taken up by Europeans like the French painter Victor Vasarely.
!!SUPREMATISM.
!Kasimir Malevich.
A Russian artist called Malevich developed a form of constructivism which he named suprematism and made primary coloured paintings on white grounds. He painted the famous ‘Black Square’ in 1910. This work was the logical if daring conclusion of his systematic attention to constructivist principles and is often attributed with marking the beginning of abstraction. It is also a stark political statement that there is nothing left to paint. 
Black square is simply a square canvas painted black all over. Square canvases were very unusual since they did not lend themselves well to the composition of traditional subjects of painting, landscapes and portraits. The square drew attention to the work itself, not the subject of the work, so that the painting as an object, about painting, rather than as a depiction of something became important. Mondrian soon began to use the square format. 
Malevich's painting was entirely black which drew attention to the only possible details of his work, the colour of the black and the brushstrokes. The viewer was directed to regard only the physicality of the paint in this work. This may have been more than Malevich intended.
! Wassily Kandinsky.
Another Russian artist, Kandinsky, is also credited with making some of the first abstract paintings. Whereas Malevich had made a minimal painting, Kandinsky used bright colours and black on a white ground to make expressive gestural flowing marks, giving rise to the term abstract expressionism. He was interested in the relation of music to sound and some of his paintings are attempts to ‘paint’ music. 
 He painted some stage backdrops for experimental orchestral works.
!!GERMAN EXPRESSIONISM.
! Ernst Ludwig Kirchner. 
Kirchner was an early German expressionist painter. His paintings are brightly coloured, with angular stylised figures, and lots of brushstrokes. Kirchner was the founder of an artists group called die Brucke, (The Bridge) which included Kandinsky among its members. Die Brucke published a magazine of critical texts called Die Blaue Reiter (The Blue Rider).
The First World War had a profound effect on artists. Oscar Kokoschka, Otto Mueller and others registered their horror at what had happened in their work.
!Max Beckman.
Beckman was a German artist who painted in an expressionist style, choosing to portray the people and social spaces of the day in Berlin. A self portrait shows him full length, standing glaring at the viewer wearing a large black overcoat and a hat with a wide brim. Beckman used a lot of black to outline his figures. 
!Otto Dix.
Dix painted first world war atrocities, including limbless and horrifically injured soldiers, the best known showing two veterans sitting at a café table, one of whom has had his leg blown off, the other has an eyeball hanging out.
!George Grosz.
Grosz painted the decadence of the Weimar republic at the beginning of the twentieth century and the aftermath of war. 
!Paul Nash.
One of the few British artists who made paintings about the war, Nash showed barren landscapes of the trenches and no-mans land in a slightly surrealist light, favouring greens and browns and populating his landscapes with skeletal shapes to create an atmosphere of death.
!Egon Schiele.
Although Schiele did not directly paint about the war, his paintings are uneasy and cheerless portraits that were in tune with the times. The young women and men in Schiele’s work are usually emaciated and have hollow faces and large eyes. His portraits usually show the whole body, sometimes more than one figure, usually naked or semi-clothed on pale backgrounds and they are painted with rough brushstrokes and a palette of white with brown, grey and black.
Throughout the early twentieth century there were plenty of artists who ignored political events and continued to make art that represented romantic, spiritual or domestic subjects. 
!Balthus.
Balthus is renowned for his domestic interiors and the dreamlike quality of his paintings of young women and girls. He used a restrained palette and the features of his female sitters are flattened and idealised. Balthus continued to paint until he was a very old man. 
!Marc Chagall.
Chagall is a French painter who also designed stained glass. He is usually associated with images of lovers and animals who are sometimes flying. His narrative paintings are executed in a faux naïf style, with soft colours, and have a sentimental quality. Chagall often painted images of love and marriage, for example one painting shows a bride and groom resting on a giant hen in the sky, with symbols of the artists Jewish faith in the background. As well hens and roosters Chagall like to paint horses, for example one painting features a white horse with large green eyes and long eyelashes. 
Chagall was commissioned to design the stained glass windows for a chapel designed by le Corbusier in the south of France.
!Raoul Dufy.
Known for his idyllic seaside scenes, Dufy used bright colours and quick gestural brushstrokes. He was commissioned to paint a mural for the head quarters of the national French electricity generating company celebrating the benefits of electric power. He made a large curved panorama showing people going about varying activities in the countryside, the city and at the sea side with electricity pylons linking the different scenes. 
!Robert Delaunay.
Delaunay made semi-abstract paintings using lots of bright, luminous colours usually in small squares. He was married to Sonia Delaunay.
!Sonia Delaunay.
As well as making light bright coloured semi-abstract paintings, Delaunay is known for her designs for woven and printed textiles. 

!Jean Arp.
Arp was a Dutch artist who worked in mixed media and painting, making a lot of wall hung reliefs and constructions using primary colours and white, and simple shapes, usually curved. Arp cut out simple shapes from sheets of timber and glued them to a board, adding other elements and painting the whole thing.
!Kees Van Dongen.
Van Dongen made semi-abstract colourful paintings, applying the paint quite thickly.
Nolde and Munch made a new kind of expressionistic landscape painting.
!Emile Nolde.
The Belgian artist Nolde's expressionistic landscapes and portraits are characterised by very bright colours and impasto. 
!Edvard Munch.
One of the most famous modern paintings, The Scream, was painted by the Norwegian artist Munch. This iconic painting has often been used in advertising and has been stolen several times.
Munch had a very distinctive painting style, which gives the appearance of a woodcut at times. He outlined his figures, often paining the background to follow the contours of the person in the painting to add to the outlining. He used strong and often acid colours in later works, but many of his earlier landscapes use soft colours and monochrome.  
In The Scream, Munch wanted to suggest both the sound of the scream and the emotion that engendered it. The figure of a screaming person dressed in black, with shocked eyes and hands to open mouth, is seen on a jetty, surrounded by concentric lines of bright colours that seem to represent the sound of the scream. 
 
Munch's subjects were either the rural Norwegian landscape or people. There is often a dreamlike quality to his work, as if nothing were quite real; the sun is often a strange colour, shadows are distorted, and elements are coloured unnaturally. The people in Munch’s work always seem to be or to feel that they are alone. 
A painting of a lake has a setting sun, the reflection of which is a thickly applied vertical yellow stripe on the water. In a lithograph called The Singer a woman dressed in black stands in a room with a piano. The only bright colour in the painting is in the woman’s red hair.
At the time The Scream was painted Freud’s theories of hysteria and the unconscious were just becoming known and also influenced the work of the surrealists. Some artists experimented more literally with the subconscious mind.
!Henri Michaux.
Michaux was a French artist who made small obsessive drawings and written works, dense with scribbling, extremely intricate patterns and landscapes and letters to anonymous recipients written in the smallest of hands, sometimes blanked out after writing. Michaux mainly used pen and ink, often brown ink and made his drawings under the influence of mescaline, which Michaux believed allowed him fuller access to his subconscious..
!M C Escher.
The German artist Escher made extremely detailed and precise drawings of fantasy interiors and architecture, using impossible perspective or optical tricks such making something appear to change from convex to concave continuously.. His work has been frequently reproduced, for example an interior with a staircase descending around four walls which never goes anywhere, a pattern of black and white doves which becomes something else, and a hand drawing itself which becomes a hand drawing itself. The endgame of Esher’s work is characteristic, everything appears to return to itself. 
Some artists made work in a naïve or faux naïve style.
!Paul Klee.
The French painter Klee made a large number of small paintings and an enormous range of works on paper, using both drawing and printmaking. Klee habitually used thin drawn lines to make geometric patterns, often repeated triangles or squares, with dots, which were coloured and often resemble patchwork. Sometimes these remained as abstract patterns, at other times Klee would draw figures, trees, buildings or animals. 
!Jan Dubuffet.
Dubuffet used sand to make his paint rough textured and painted large simple figures and faces which owed something to African art but also to graffiti.
!Auguste Maillol.
Maillol's bronze and stone sculptures have solid and rounded forms, as if they cannot escape from the block from which they are carved. The bodies are distinctively simply formed and even the smaller works have a gentle monumental quality.
